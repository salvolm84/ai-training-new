Module 16 · Part IV — Shipping it

Uncertainty: how sure is the model?

Every prediction in this course so far has been a single number. You would not accept a measurement without an error bar, and you should not accept a model output without one either. This module is about where that error bar comes from, which methods give an honest one, and how to tell when it is lying to you.

The point, in one sentence

A useful model says two things: its best guess, and how far to trust that guess for this particular input. The second is harder to produce, more often missing, and usually the thing the decision actually depends on.

16.1 · Two kinds of not knowing

Run the same car, same setup, same driver, ten laps in a row, and the lap time still moves by a few tenths: a gust in the fast corner, a slightly different line, tyres a lap older, track rubbering in. That scatter is aleatoric uncertainty — variation in the thing itself. More data tells you how big it is. It does not make it smaller.

Now ask the model for the lap time at a wing angle nobody has tested. It will give you a number, but that number is a guess, because there is no evidence there. That is epistemic uncertainty — the model's ignorance. Unlike the first kind, it shrinks when you collect data in the right place.

The distinction matters because the two call for opposite actions. Epistemic uncertainty says test more, here — or do not use the model here. Aleatoric uncertainty says no amount of testing will help: tighten the process, or design for the spread.

Epistemic: buy it down with data

It lives in the gaps and beyond the edges of what you tested. It answers the question where should the next test go? — which is exactly the infill rule from module 11.3, now with a proper reason behind it. It is also the uncertainty that becomes dangerous when a model is used outside its envelope (module 11.5).

Aleatoric: design for it

It sets a floor on how well any model can predict a single lap, however much data you have. Tightening it is a test-process problem (same tyres, same fuel, same driver), not a modelling problem. Where it is large, the useful output is a distribution, not a point.

The boundary between the two is a modelling choice

Track temperature changes lap time. If you do not record it, its effect shows up as scatter and looks aleatoric. Record it and add it to the model, and the same variation becomes explained — and the uncertainty about its effect becomes epistemic. A lot of what teams call “noise” is a variable nobody logged.

So when someone says “that's just noise”, the useful follow-up is: noise with respect to which inputs? Sometimes the cheapest way to shrink an error bar is a sensor, not a model.

  • About 6 minutes. Start on “Gap in the middle” with the probe at 10°: the blue band balloons in the gap. Then switch to “Even spread” and watch it collapse. Then drag the scatter up: the orange band grows everywhere and no plan fixes it.
  • Push the lap count to 120 with the gap plan. The epistemic band in the gap barely moves — more laps in the wrong place buy nothing. That usually lands well with test engineers.
  • Expected question: “so which one is bigger in practice?” Honest answer: inside a well-tested envelope, usually aleatoric; at the edges and in new configurations, epistemic, by a lot.
  • Expected question: “is 25 models expensive?” Here they are tiny. Section 16.3 deals with the cost for real networks.

16.2 · Gaussian processes: a surrogate that knows where it has been

Module 11 built surrogates that gave one number everywhere and no warning. A Gaussian process — the same thing the optimisation tools call kriging — fixes that. Instead of fitting one curve, it starts from a prior over smooth functions and conditions it on your runs. What comes out is a mean (the surrogate) and a variance that is small near the runs and grows back to the prior far from them.

The assumption about smoothness lives in the kernel. The usual choice says two inputs closer than about one length-scale ℓ behave alike:

k(x, x′) = s² · exp( −(x − x′)² / 2ℓ² )

What it gets right

The band is narrow where you have runs and wide where you do not, which is exactly the warning module 11.5 said was missing. It also gives the optimiser something principled to chase: “sample where the mean plus two sigma is highest” is the exploration bonus from 11.3, done properly. Expected improvement, which most commercial tools use, is a refinement of the same idea.

What it gets wrong

The band is only as honest as the kernel. Below 28 mm the GP's band widens, but it is centred on a smooth continuation, because nothing told it a stall exists. Look at the coverage outside the tested range: a wide band is not the same as a correct band. A 95% claim is a calibration claim (module 8.4), and you check it on held-out runs, not by looking at the plot.

  • About 8 minutes; this is the centrepiece. Start with 7 runs, then click once or twice in the widest part of the band. Each click is “one CFD run”; the room sees the band pinch to the dot.
  • Then wreck it: ℓ = 1.5 (every millimetre independent — mean collapses between runs), then ℓ = 35 with σₙ tiny (cannot fit — or fits with absurd confidence). Then press the maximum-likelihood button and let it recover. That is what the software does for them silently.
  • Click a run at 22 mm. The GP discovers the stall only because you looked. Make the point that nothing in the model would have told you to look there except the width of the band.
  • Expected question: “does this scale?” Cost is n³ — fine to a few thousand runs, which covers most CFD and rig work. Beyond that there are sparse approximations; or use section 16.3.
  • Expected question: “more than one input?” Same maths, one length-scale per input. The fitted length-scales are a useful sensitivity readout in their own right.

16.3 · Ensembles: the practical method for networks and trees

Gaussian processes do not scale to millions of training points or to images. For neural networks, the method that works best in practice is almost embarrassingly simple: train several, and look at how much they disagree. Five networks with different random starting weights — a “deep ensemble” — remains one of the strongest uncertainty baselines in the literature. For trees you get it nearly free: a random forest already is an ensemble of bootstrapped trees.

Where the members have seen data, they are forced to agree. Where they have not, each one fills the gap in its own way, and the spread becomes an uncertainty signal. Whether that signal grows in the right places depends on the model family — which is what this lab is for.

Disagreement is a signal, not a measurement

Nothing calibrates the spread. Two standard deviations of disagreement is not a 95% interval unless you check it on held-out data — or wrap it in conformal prediction, section 16.4. Use it to rank inputs from “familiar” to “unfamiliar”; do not quote it as an error bar raw.

Trees go flat, and agree

Outside its data a tree predicts the value of its last leaf, and every bootstrapped tree has much the same last leaf. The forest reports its highest confidence where it is most wrong. For extrapolation risk, pair tree ensembles with an explicit envelope check.

What it costs

K times the training and K times the inference. Five members is usually enough; the gain from ten is small. Cheaper approximations exist (Monte Carlo dropout, snapshot ensembles); they are generally weaker, and worth using only when five full models are unaffordable.

  • About 6 minutes. Let the networks train in front of the room — it takes a couple of seconds. Point at the lower plot: outside 38 °C the blue spread and the red error both rise. Then switch to trees: the red line climbs, the blue line stays flat. That contrast is the slide.
  • Press “Different random seeds” a few times on the network setting. The band changes shape. That is honest: with six members the estimate of the spread is itself noisy.
  • Expected question: “random forests give a variance — isn't that the same?” It is this, and it has this weakness. Quantile regression forests are better for aleatoric spread, not for extrapolation.
  • Do not oversell the networks either: even they cover the truth only part of the time outside the range. The spread grows; it does not grow enough. Hence 16.4.

16.4 · Conformal prediction: intervals with a guarantee

Everything so far produces a spread. None of it guarantees anything. Conformal prediction does, under one assumption, and it is three lines of code wrapped around whatever model you already have:

1. hold out a calibration set the model never trained on, and score each case: si = | yi − ŷ(xi) |
2. q̂ = the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest score   ·   3. interval = ŷ(x) ± q̂ if new cases are exchangeable with the calibration cases, the interval contains the truth at least (1 − α) of the time — for any model, any n, no Gaussian assumption

That last condition is the whole deal. It holds when tomorrow's cases come from the same process as the calibration set. It says nothing at all when they do not.

Valid on average, not per region

With the absolute score, the bins tell the story: at low speed the interval is far too wide, at high speed too narrow, and the two average out to exactly the target. The guarantee is marginal — over all cases, not for any particular region or case. Scaling the score by a spread model (even a crude one) fixes most of this. Always look at coverage by region, not just the headline.

Exchangeability is the whole assumption

Move the shift slider: coverage falls, and nothing in the interval tells you. A new circuit, a new compound, a new supplier's disc — each is a new distribution. Conformal prediction needs an envelope check and drift monitoring beside it (module 19), and a recalibration on data from the new conditions before its number means anything there.

  • About 8 minutes. Start at 90%, absolute score, no shift. Point at “coverage, same circuit”: it lands on target. Press “Repeat 500 times” to show it is not luck — the average sits at or just above the target, with real scatter when the calibration set is small.
  • Then the bins. This is the slide for the statisticians in the room: correct on average, wrong everywhere. Switch to the scaled score and the bars flatten.
  • Then the shift, back on the absolute score. At 20 km/h it barely moves; by 40 it is visibly below target and by 60 well below. The intervals look identical; the coverage is not. The scaled score degrades more slowly, because its spread model happens to extrapolate in the right direction — luck, not a guarantee.
  • Drop calibration cases to 10: at 90%, q̂ is the largest score seen; at 95% it becomes infinite. (n + 1)(1 − α) must not exceed n — you need roughly 1/α cases before a 1 − α claim is even possible.
  • Expected question: “which model should we wrap?” Any. A better model gives narrower intervals; the coverage guarantee holds for a bad one too, which is the point.

16.5 · Reporting uncertainty: to a decision maker, and to certification

Two audiences, two different needs. The decision maker wants to know whether the requirement is met and what the plausible worst case is. Certification wants every source of error separated, quantified by a stated method, and traceable. Both are served by the same artefact metrologists have used for decades: an uncertainty budget — list each contribution, quantify it, combine them, and say which one dominates.

Saying it to a decision maker

  • A range with a probability, in their units: “3,840 counts, 95% interval 3,770 to 3,910; about a 90% chance of meeting the 3,800 requirement.” Not “the model is 95% confident”.
  • Lead with the decision-relevant number — the probability of meeting the requirement, or of the new part being better than the old — not a standard deviation.
  • Say what is not included. An interval that leaves out model-form error is not a small interval; it is an incomplete one.
  • Say what would shrink it, and what that costs. The budget tells you: the largest bar is where the next euro goes.

Saying it to certification

  • Surrogate error is its own line, separate from solver error, as module 11.10 said. It is measured on held-out solver runs (coverage, as in 16.2 and 16.4), not inferred.
  • Each line names its method and evidence: grid-convergence study for discretisation, correlation campaign against tunnel or track for model form, repeat runs for repeatability, a drawing tolerance for inputs.
  • The validity envelope is stated, and the budget is only claimed inside it.
  • Correlations are argued, not assumed away. When comparing two designs on the same solver, model-form error largely cancels; when comparing CFD to track, it does not.
  • About 5 minutes. At 40 mm the budget is dominated by model form. Ask the room what they would spend money on: almost always someone says “more CFD runs”, and the bars say a correlation campaign instead.
  • Then move the ride height to about 30 mm: the tolerance term becomes the largest, because the curve is steep there. Raise the tolerance to 1.5 mm and the histogram grows a long left tail — some cars are reaching the stall. A standard deviation stops being a fair summary; report the interval and the probability, which are computed from the samples, not from a Gaussian assumption.
  • Be explicit that the four source sizes are authored. The arithmetic and the propagation are real.
  • Expected question: “does anyone actually do this for ML models?” In regulated industries, increasingly yes — and it is the same budget you already produce for test results. The ML model adds one line; it does not change the method.

16.6 · Common traps

The biggest one first, because it is built into almost every classifier you will be shown: the number that comes out of the softmax (or sigmoid) is called a probability, and it is routinely read as “how sure the model is”. It is not. It measures which side of the boundary the input falls, and how far. Far from any training data, a network is usually more confident, not less.

Softmax confidence is not uncertainty

As above. Even when it is well calibrated in-distribution (module 8.4, temperature scaling), calibration says nothing about inputs unlike the training data. Ask for disagreement, distance, or a separate out-of-distribution check.

Valid on average, not per region

The bins in 16.4. A 90% interval that is 99% in easy regions and 70% in the hard ones is “correct” and useless where it matters. Always report coverage broken down by the operating conditions you care about.

The wrong interval

A confidence interval on the mean lap time shrinks with √n and goes to zero. A prediction interval on the next lap never goes below the scatter. Reports mix them up constantly, nearly always in the flattering direction. Ask which one you are looking at.

Inputs treated as exact

A model with a perfect error bar, fed a ride height that is only known to ±1 mm, still has the ±1 mm problem. Propagate input tolerance (16.5) — near a cliff it is the biggest term.

Assuming a Gaussian

“±2σ” only means 95% for symmetric, well-behaved errors. Near stalls, limits and saturation the distribution is skewed; report percentiles from samples, not a standard deviation.

Ensembles that are not really independent

Five copies of one model fine-tuned from the same checkpoint on the same data will agree with each other and with their shared mistakes. Diversity — different seeds, data resamples, ideally different model families — is what makes the spread mean something.

And for language models

“I am fairly confident that…” is generated text, not a measurement (module 8.4). The closest thing to an ensemble for an LLM is to ask the same question several times and check whether the answers agree; disagreement is a useful warning, agreement is not proof. For anything that matters, the uncertainty has to come from checking against a source, not from the model's tone.

  • About 5 minutes. Wait for the five networks to finish (a second or two). The probe starts in the bottom left: low vibration and low temperature, which is roughly what a disconnected sensor reads. Network 1 says “healthy” at 99% or more; the members split. That is the whole trap in one click — and a real failure mode.
  • Be honest about the right-hand map: some regions far from the data are still calm, because all five networks happened to extend their boundaries the same way. That is why the distance readout is there too. Disagreement catches much of the unfamiliar, not all of it; the five-line envelope check from 11.5 catches the rest.
  • The “wrong interval” card is the one most likely to be found in their own reports. It is worth saying slowly.

The minimum to ask for, every time
  1. Every prediction comes with an interval, and the report says which kind (prediction or confidence) and what it includes.
  2. Coverage is checked on held-out data, overall and per operating region.
  3. The envelope is stated and enforced; outside it, the interval is not claimed.
  4. Surrogate error is a separate line in the uncertainty budget.
  5. Someone owns recalibration when conditions change.
