Module 20 · Part IV — Shipping it

AI in safety-critical vehicle systems

Everything so far has been about getting a model to work. This module is about the much harder claim that it is safe when it fails — on a vehicle, at speed, with someone in it. The automotive safety world has a mature answer for conventional software. Machine learning breaks several of its assumptions, and the industry is rebuilding them in public.

The argument of the module, in one paragraph

You cannot prove a learned component correct, because there is no specification to prove it against and the input space is too large to test exhaustively. So you do three things instead: bound where it is used (the operational design domain), contain what it does when it is wrong (architecture: monitors, fallbacks, degradation), and assemble an honest argument from many partial pieces of evidence (the safety case). No single piece is sufficient. Most of the pieces are things earlier modules already taught you to produce.

20.1 · Why machine learning breaks the classic assumptions

Conventional functional safety rests on a chain that works well for code an engineer wrote: hazards are analysed, safety requirements are derived from them, requirements are refined into a design, the design is implemented, and every step is verified against the one above it. Failures are either random (hardware wears out, a bit flips) or systematic (someone made a mistake in a requirement or the code) — and the process is built to drive both down. Each link of that chain weakens when the behaviour is learned rather than written.

There is no specification to verify against

“Detect pedestrians” is not a specification. Nobody can write down, in a form code could be checked against, what a pedestrian looks like in every lighting, pose, occlusion and costume. That inability is why we used machine learning in the first place — and it means the classic “verify the implementation against the requirement” step has nothing precise to hold on to.

Behaviour comes from data, not from design

The model's behaviour is a consequence of the training set, the labels and the optimiser. A gap in the data (module 7) is a design defect that no code review will find, because it is not in the code. The dataset becomes a safety-relevant work product in its own right — which is exactly what ISO/PAS 8800 says (section 20.2).

Exhaustive testing is impossible

A camera frame has millions of input dimensions. You test a vanishingly small sample of the input space and infer the rest. That inference is statistical (module 8) — and section 20.6 shows how quickly the numbers become absurd if road testing is the only evidence.

And two further problems

  • The model works fine and the function is still unsafe. A detector meeting its accuracy target can still miss the one class of object that matters in a given scenario. Nothing has failed; the intended function was insufficient. Classic functional safety is about malfunctions; this is a different category, and it has its own standard (SOTIF, section 20.3).
  • Confidence is not correctness. A network outside its training distribution does not become hesitant; it becomes confidently wrong (module 11.5, module 16). You cannot rely on the component to tell you when to stop trusting it.

What does not change

  • Hazard analysis and risk assessment at vehicle level — the ML component inherits its safety requirements from the function it serves, like any other element.
  • Random hardware faults on the chip the model runs on are still handled by ISO 26262 in the normal way.
  • The software around the model — pre-processing, post-processing, the runtime, the monitor — is conventional software and is developed and verified conventionally.
  • The obligation to argue, with evidence, that residual risk is acceptable.
  • ~5 min. Open with a question: “who here has worked on an ISO 26262 project?” In this audience it will be a good fraction of the room. They are the experts for this module; you are bringing the ML half.
  • The single most useful sentence: “the classic chain assumes you can write the requirement down. We use ML precisely when we can't.” Everything else follows.
  • Expected question: “so you can never use ML in an ASIL function?” Answer: not the ML on its own, with today's methods, as the thing that carries the safety requirement — but it can sit inside an architecture that does carry it (20.5). Do not over-promise either way.

20.2 · The standards landscape, and how it stacks

Four layers, from law down to engineering practice. They are complementary rather than competing: each later standard explicitly builds on the earlier ones rather than replacing them.

Orientation, not legal advice — and this section dates quickly

Status checked against public sources in October 2026 (listed at the end of the page). Two of the standards below are under revision, a new UN regulation was adopted in June 2026, and the AI Act was amended in July 2026. Re-check every line before each delivery of this seminar, and take actual compliance questions to your homologation, functional-safety and legal colleagues — they own these processes.

EU lawWhat you must do to sell

Vehicle type approval — Regulation (EU) 2018/858 is the framework; Regulation (EU) 2019/2144, the General Safety Regulation, mandates specific driver-assistance systems (intelligent speed assistance, emergency braking, emergency lane keeping, drowsiness warning…) — several of which are, in practice, camera-and-ML systems already. Regulation (EU) 2022/1426 covers type approval of automated driving systems for fully automated vehicles in small series.

The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, amended in 2026) — and for vehicles it takes an unusual route. See the box below.

UNECERegulations type approval references

UN Regulations under the 1958 Agreement, adopted at the WP.29 World Forum and referenced by EU type approval. The ones that touch ML-based functions most directly: R155 (cyber-security management system), R156 (software update management — relevant because every retrained model you ship is a software update), R157 (automated lane keeping, SAE level 3), and R171 (driver control assistance systems, level 2).

In June 2026 WP.29 adopted a new UN Regulation and a Global Technical Regulation on automated driving systems (level 3 and above), built around a safety management system, a structured safety case and in-service monitoring rather than a fixed list of test procedures. The EU has signalled it intends to bring it into its type-approval framework.

ISO 26262Functional safety · 2018, 2nd ed.

Hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of E/E systems — random hardware faults and systematic faults. Hazard analysis assigns an ASIL (A lowest to D highest, from severity, exposure and controllability), or QM when ordinary quality management suffices. The ASIL sets the rigour of everything downstream.

A third edition is in preparation (several parts at Draft International Standard stage in 2026, publication widely expected in 2027), with more guidance on ML. Until it is published, 26262:2018 is the edition you work to.

ISO 21448SOTIF · 2022

Safety of the intended functionality: hazards that occur when nothing has failed, because the intended function or its implementation is insufficient — a sensor that cannot see through spray, a detector that has never seen a person carrying a mirror. Organised around triggering conditions and the four scenario areas in section 20.3.

A revision (edition 2) is at working-draft stage. 21448:2022 is current.

ISO/PAS 8800Safety and AI · Dec 2024

Road vehicles — Safety and artificial intelligence. Published December 2024 as a Publicly Available Specification (a step short of a full International Standard). It extends 26262 and 21448 to AI elements rather than replacing them: it covers both malfunctions and functional insufficiencies of AI components, and defines an AI safety lifecycle — derivation of AI safety requirements, design, verification and validation, and safety analysis of the AI element, through to operation.

The point engineers notice first: it treats data as a safety-relevant work product. Datasets have requirements, design, verification and change control, just as code does. Module 7 stops being good practice and becomes a deliverable.

Alongside these, worth knowing the names

ISO 34503 — a taxonomy for describing an ODD (section 20.4). ISO 34502 — scenario-based safety evaluation for automated driving. UL 4600 — a standard for the safety case of autonomous products, unusually explicit about what a credible argument must contain. ISO/IEC TR 5469 — a cross-industry technical report on functional safety and AI. None replaces the automotive stack; each is useful when building the evidence for it.

How the AI Act reaches a vehicle

Module 10 introduced the two routes into high-risk under the AI Act. Vehicles are on the Annex I route — but specifically Section B of Annex I, which lists the vehicle type-approval regulations (2018/858, 2019/2144, and the two- and three-wheeler and agricultural-vehicle regulations) alongside aviation and rail.

For Section B products, Article 2(2) says that only a short list of the Act's articles apply directly. The substantive high-risk requirements — risk management, data governance, accuracy and robustness, human oversight (Chapter III, Section 2) — reach vehicles indirectly: Articles 107 and 109 amend 2018/858 and 2019/2144 so that when the Commission adopts delegated or implementing acts on AI systems that are safety components, it must take those requirements into account.

In plain terms: for an AI safety component of a car, AI Act compliance is expected to arrive through type approval, written into vehicle regulations, assessed by the type-approval authority — not as a separate AI Act conformity assessment running in parallel.

What that means for you, cautiously

  • Module 10's 2 August 2028 date is the deferred application date for Annex I high-risk systems generally. For vehicles, the date that matters in practice is when the type-approval acts that bring the requirements in are adopted and apply. As of October 2026 I could not confirm that any such act has been adopted — watch for them.
  • The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) amended the Act, including moving machinery from Section A to Section B of Annex I — which matters for plant equipment, not just vehicles.
  • Everything off the vehicle — HR tools, office assistants, workplace monitoring — is unaffected by this route and is classified exactly as in module 10.
  • Being outside the Act's direct obligations is not a lighter regime. Type approval plus 26262, 21448 and 8800 is considerably more demanding than most of the AI Act.

The exact wording of Article 2(2) was itself touched by the 2026 amendment. Read the consolidated text with your legal function rather than relying on this summary.

  • ~8 min. Do not read the stack aloud. Point at the colours: law says whether; UN regulations say what is assessed; 26262, 21448 and 8800 say how you engineer it.
  • The ISO 26262 people will know the top half better than you. Let them fill in; your contribution is the 8800 layer and the data-as-work-product point.
  • Expected question: “does the AI Act apply to our ADAS or not?” Honest answer: its requirements are meant to arrive via type approval rather than directly, the implementing acts are what to watch, and the homologation team is who to ask. Do not give a yes/no from the stage.
  • Expected question: “is 8800 mandatory?” It is a standard, not law. Like 26262, it becomes de facto expected as the state of the art that an assessor and a court will compare you against.
  • Before each delivery: re-check the 26262 edition 3 and 21448 edition 2 status, and whether any type-approval act implementing Articles 107/109 has been adopted.

20.3 · SOTIF: shrinking the unknown unsafe

ISO 21448 sorts every scenario the function might meet into four areas, by two questions: is it known (identified and evaluated) or unknown, and is the function's behaviour in it safe or unsafe?

  • Area 1 — known safe. You have found it, evaluated it, and the function handles it.
  • Area 2 — known unsafe. You know about it and it is not yet handled. Reduced by improving the function, adding a sensor, restricting the ODD, or warning the driver.
  • Area 3 — unknown unsafe. The dangerous one: scenarios nobody has thought of where the function misbehaves. Reduced only by discovering them — analysis, simulation, testing — which moves them into area 2, where they can be fixed.
  • Area 4 — unknown safe. Harmless, and you will never know about most of it.

The SOTIF activities aim to make areas 2 and 3 small enough that the residual risk is acceptable. The lab makes that concrete.

How this works: a seeded population of 1,500 synthetic scenarios, each with an exposure (how often it occurs per million km, log-uniform over five decades), a foreseeability (how easily an analyst would think of it), whether the simulator can represent it, a probability of being hit per simulation run, and whether the function is unsafe in it (more likely for unforeseeable ones). Each method discovers a scenario with a probability that grows with effort; the four areas are counted, not drawn. The distributions and rates are authored for teaching, not calibrated to any real function.

What the lab shows

Road testing finds the frequent scenarios — which is why the exposure-weighted view clears quickly while the count view hardly moves. What remains in area 3 is the long tail: individually rare, collectively not negligible, and practically invisible to road testing. Analysis and simulation are what reach into the tail.

The honest limit

You can never measure area 3 directly — by definition you do not know what is in it. You estimate it from the rate at which new unsafe scenarios are still being discovered. A discovery rate that is still climbing is evidence the function is not ready, whatever the accuracy figure says.

  • ~7 min. Start with everything near zero, then raise road testing alone to the maximum. Count view: area 3 barely shrinks. Then switch to exposure: it nearly vanishes. That contrast is the whole lesson — testing finds what is common, and the danger lives in what is rare.
  • Then raise analysis and simulation and show the bars: they find different scenarios from road testing. Diversity of method, like diversity of architecture in 20.5.
  • Expected question: “how do you know when area 3 is small enough?” You don't, directly. You argue it from the discovery curve flattening, from coverage of the ODD, and from field monitoring after release (module 19). Say this plainly.
  • Be explicit that the numbers are synthetic. The shape is the point.

20.4 · The operational design domain

The ODD is the set of conditions under which a function is designed to operate: speeds, road types, weather, lighting, temperature, geography, traffic, time of day. It is the single most powerful safety lever available, because it converts an impossible claim (“safe everywhere”) into a bounded one (“safe on dry motorways in daylight between 60 and 130 km/h”) — and the function must recognise when it is leaving the ODD and hand over or degrade.

For an ML component there are two different ways to be outside your evidence. Out of ODD: the vehicle is somewhere the function was never meant to operate; the monitor should catch this. In ODD but under-covered: the conditions are inside what you declared, but the training and test data contain too few examples like it to support any claim. The second is the dangerous one, because nothing stops the function running. It is the envelope problem from module 11.5, with a vehicle attached.

How this works: 6,000 seeded synthetic training samples drawn from a distribution skewed the way real collection campaigns are (mostly motorway, clear, daylight, mild). Incoming scenarios come from a broader “field” distribution with more urban driving, night and bad weather. A scenario counts as covered when at least the chosen number of training samples share its road, weather and lighting and lie within ±15 km/h and ±6 °C. The distributions are authored; the checking is computed live.

Three rules that follow
  1. Declare the ODD from the evidence, not from the product brief. If the data cannot support night-time urban driving, it is not in the ODD, whatever the feature list says.
  2. Every ODD dimension needs a runtime monitor — a way for the vehicle to know it has left: speed and temperature are easy, “heavy rain” and “fog” need their own detection, and that detection is itself a safety-relevant function.
  3. Coverage is per cell, not per dimension. Plenty of rain and plenty of night does not mean any night-time rain. Combinations are where data runs out first.
  • ~7 min. Load “Marketing ODD” first: almost nothing is out of ODD (great availability) and a large red block is under-covered. Then “Motorway pilot”: much is out of ODD, and what is in is covered. That is the trade the product team and the safety team will argue about.
  • Point at the cell table: the coverage collapses on combinations — night × heavy rain, urban × snow. Ask who has seen a data-collection campaign plan that addressed combinations explicitly.
  • Expected question: “how many samples is enough?” There is no universal number; it comes from the performance requirement and the statistics of module 8 and section 20.6. The slider is there to show the answer is a decision, and must be justified.

20.5 · Architectures that make ML safe enough

If you cannot make the learned component trustworthy enough on its own, you build a system in which it does not have to be. These patterns are old — aviation and rail used most of them long before machine learning — and they are where most of the practical safety of ML-based vehicle functions comes from.

Doer / checker (safety monitor, safety cage)

The ML component (the doer) proposes; a simpler, deterministic, verifiable component (the checker) decides whether the proposal is within safe bounds — a physically plausible trajectory, a deceleration within limits, an object list consistent with radar. The checker carries the ASIL; the doer can be developed to a lower integrity. The checker does not need to be able to drive; it needs only to recognise unsafe output.

Redundancy and diversity

Two channels that fail independently. Two copies of the same network trained on the same data are not independent — they fail on the same inputs. Diversity means different sensors (camera and radar), different principles (learned and rule-based), different data. The quantity to argue about is the common-cause fraction: the share of failures both channels share.

Degradation modes and fallback

When the checker rejects the output, or the vehicle leaves the ODD, something deterministic takes over: a reduced function, a handover to the driver with a defined time budget, or a minimal-risk manoeuvre. Every intervention costs availability — the feature switches off — and that cost is what drives people to loosen the checker. The lab below is that negotiation.

Keep ML out of the ASIL path

The simplest pattern, and the one most production vehicles use today: ML in functions rated QM or low ASIL — comfort, convenience, information, perception that a deterministic function cross-checks — with a deterministic path for anything that can hurt someone. Unglamorous, defensible, and shippable now.

How this works: the checker evaluates a plausibility score 10 times a second (36,000 checks per hour). For normal doer outputs the score is standard-normal noise; for hazardous outputs it is shifted by the separation — except for the common-cause fraction, which the checker cannot distinguish from normal. Above the threshold the checker intervenes and the deterministic fallback runs for 30 s (fallback assumed to fail once in 10,000 demands). Rates come from exact normal tail probabilities. The two things the lab teaches

First, a good checker turns a mediocre doer into a much safer system — if it is discriminating enough to do so without stopping the feature every few minutes. Second, and more important: no threshold beats the common-cause floor. If the checker shares the doer's camera, its blind spots or its training data, the failures they share go straight through. Independence is the property to argue for and evidence, not assume.

  • ~7 min. Start at the defaults, then slide the threshold left: hazards drop, availability collapses — the feature is useless. Slide right: availability is fine, hazards come back. Then the payoff: raise common-cause to 20% and show that the curve hits a floor no threshold can get under.
  • The engineering question to leave with them: “what does your checker share with your doer?” Same sensor, same pre-processing, same assumptions about the world, same people.
  • Expected question: “so who builds the checker?” Ideally a different team with a different method. That organisational independence is itself an argument in the safety case.

20.6 · The statistics of proving it

Suppose you want to demonstrate, by driving, that a function's rate of a given failure is below some target λ. If you drive a distance n and see no failures, the classical result for a Poisson process is:

n = −ln(1 − C) / λ failure-free distance (or hours) needed to show the rate is below λ with confidence C. At 95 %, −ln(0.05) ≈ 3: the “rule of three”. With k failures observed, the factor grows (Poisson / chi-square).

Why road testing alone cannot carry the argument

RAND's well-known 2016 analysis put the distance needed to show, at 95% confidence, a fatality rate no worse than human drivers at about 275 million failure-free miles, and to show it was 20% better at around 8.8 billion miles. And every change to the ML component — a retrain, a new dataset — weakens the claim that past kilometres still count.

Why simulation is necessary and not sufficient

Simulation makes the per-scenario arithmetic affordable, and lets you sample the rare cases deliberately. But a simulation result is only evidence if the simulator is validated for that scenario — sensor models, physics, other agents. That validity is itself a claim with its own evidence (modules 11.6 and 11.10). The working answer is a blend: analysis to find scenarios, simulation to cover them, proving-ground tests to validate the simulator, road miles to catch what nobody imagined.

  • ~6 min. Load the human fatality rate and read the number aloud. Then set the fleet to something realistic for a manufacturer's test fleet and read the calendar time. Then set two observed failures — the requirement jumps — and point out that every software release arguably resets it.
  • Switch to per-hour and load 10⁻⁸/h: around 300 million failure-free operating hours. That is the intuition behind why nobody demonstrates ASIL D integrity by test.
  • The per-scenario panel is the constructive turn: same statistics, but simulation runs cost seconds, not kilometres. Then immediately add the caveat about simulator validity — the engineers in the room will raise it if you do not.
  • Expected question: “so how does anyone release an automated driving system?” With a safety case combining all of the above, a restricted ODD, conservative architecture, and in-service monitoring — which is exactly what the 2026 UN regulation asks for.

20.7 · The safety case for an ML component

A safety case is a structured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is acceptably safe for a given application in a given environment. Three parts: claims (what you assert), arguments (why the evidence supports the claim), and evidence (the artefacts). For an ML component, no single piece of evidence carries the claim; the strength is in the combination, and in being explicit about what is still missing.

This is where the whole course comes together. Nearly every earlier module produces a piece of evidence: the data work of module 7, the evaluation of module 8, the uncertainty estimates of module 16, the explanations of module 17, the monitoring of module 19.

What reviewers look for first

Not the accuracy figure. They look for the ODD definition, the data specification and its coverage of that ODD, performance broken down by slice rather than averaged, and what the architecture does when the model is wrong. A safety case built on one aggregate test-set number is a performance report, not a safety case.

  • ~6 min. Load “Good ML team, no safety process” — it is the most realistic preset for most companies starting out, and the gaps it shows are not ML gaps; they are ODD, architecture and field-monitoring gaps.
  • Make the course-wide point: the evidence on this page is what modules 7, 8, 16, 17 and 19 taught people to produce. The safety case is the reason to produce it properly.
  • Expected question: “who writes the safety case — the ML team or the safety team?” Both; the argument is owned by safety, the evidence is produced by the ML team, and they must start together.

20.8 · Where none of this is needed

A module like this can leave the impression that AI in a car company is a regulatory minefield. It is not. The heavy machinery of this page applies to ML that is on the vehicle and in a safety-relevant path. Almost everything else is governed by the ordinary rules of modules 8 and 10 — and that is where most of the value is today.

Fine today, with ordinary good practice

  • Engineering tools: simulation surrogates, design-space exploration, test-data analysis, requirements and document search (module 11). The validated method makes the decision; ML accelerates getting there.
  • Plant: visual inspection, predictive maintenance, scheduling, energy optimisation (modules 6, 15). Machinery-safety rules still apply where ML touches a safety function of a machine — and note that machinery moved into Annex I Section B in 2026.
  • Office: drafting, summarising, coding assistants, knowledge retrieval (modules 9, 12), under the data rules of module 10.5.
  • Validation support: ML that finds scenarios to test, mines fleet data for triggering conditions, or prioritises test cases. It strengthens the safety process without being part of the safety function — though if its output is used as safety evidence, the tool itself needs qualification.

On the vehicle: the rest of this page applies

  • QM functions: infotainment, voice assistant, comfort, personalisation. Not safety functions — but check the hazard analysis says so, keep them isolated from safety functions, and note R155/R156 still apply to the software.
  • ADAS with ML perception: emergency braking, lane keeping, speed-limit recognition. ASIL-rated, safety-relevant, and the main home of ISO/PAS 8800 today.
  • Chassis and powertrain: learned estimators for tyre grip, battery state, vehicle state — often feeding safety functions indirectly, which is easy to overlook. Trace where the output goes.
  • Automated driving: the full stack of 20.2 to 20.7, plus the 2026 UN ADS regulation.
The question to ask of any proposal

“If this output is wrong, can anyone get hurt — and if so, through which path?” If the answer is no, use module 8. If the answer is yes, involve the functional-safety team before the architecture is fixed, not after the model works.

  • ~4 min. End on the positive: the large majority of the value available in the next two years is in the left-hand column, and none of it needs a safety case.
  • The indirect-path point in the right column is the one that catches people: a learned battery or grip estimate that ends up as an input to a stability or torque function is safety-relevant, even if nobody called it that.
