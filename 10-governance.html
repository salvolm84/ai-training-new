Module 10 · Part II — Engineering it

Governance, security and the AI Act

The least popular module and one of the two or three most important. For a European manufacturer this is not a compliance afterthought — it changes what you are allowed to build, what you must document while building it, and who signs.

Read this first

I am not a lawyer and this module is not legal advice. It is an engineer's orientation map, accurate as of September 2026, intended to help you ask your legal, privacy and compliance colleagues the right questions early rather than late. The law has already been amended once during its transition period — see section 10.3 — so verify every date and obligation with your own legal function before acting, and re-check before each delivery of this seminar.

10.1 · Why an engineer should care

It is product legislation

The AI Act is built like CE-marking law, not like data-protection law. If you have ever been near type approval or machinery conformity, the shape is familiar: risk classification, technical documentation, conformity assessment, post-market monitoring, incident reporting. That is engineering work, and it happens during development.

Documentation is not retrofittable

The obligations ask for records of data governance, testing, accuracy and human oversight — the module 7 and module 8 artefacts. Produced as you go, they are nearly free. Reconstructed a year later for an assessment, they are expensive and sometimes impossible.

Most of it is good practice anyway

Read the high-risk obligations next to the module 8 evaluation checklist and the overlap is striking. A team that evaluates honestly, logs properly and keeps a human in the loop is most of the way there for reasons that have nothing to do with law.

10.2 · Four tiers, and where you probably sit

The Act classifies by use, not by technology. The same model can be unregulated in one application and high-risk in another — which means the classification question belongs to whoever defines the use case, not to whoever builds the model.

0

Prohibited

A short list of practices banned outright, applicable since 2 February 2025. Includes social scoring, manipulative techniques that exploit vulnerabilities, untargeted scraping of facial images, inferring sensitive characteristics from biometrics, and — directly relevant to employers — emotion recognition in the workplace, outside narrow safety and medical exceptions.

The workplace emotion-recognition ban catches more well-intentioned product ideas than people expect: driver or operator “engagement” monitoring, sentiment scoring of employee communications, interview analysis.

1

High-risk

Permitted, with substantial obligations. Two routes in:

  • Annex I — AI that is a safety component of a product already covered by EU harmonisation legislation: vehicles, machinery, lifts, PPE, medical devices, toys. For an automotive engineering organisation this is the route that matters.
  • Annex III — eight listed areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment (recruitment, task allocation, monitoring, promotion, termination), essential private and public services including credit and insurance, law enforcement, migration, and justice.

Note that ordinary HR use of AI — CV screening, candidate ranking, allocating work — lands in Annex III. It is the most common way a manufacturer acquires a high-risk system without intending to.

2

Transparency obligations

Systems that interact with people or generate content. People must be told they are dealing with an AI; synthetic audio, image, video and text must be machine-readably marked; deepfakes must be disclosed. These apply from 2 August 2026, with a later date for systems that were already on the market.

This is the tier most internal chat assistants and content tools land in. The obligations are light, and they are obligations.

3

Minimal risk

Everything else — predictive maintenance, defect detection on parts, demand forecasting, simulation surrogates, document search. No specific obligations under the Act beyond the general AI-literacy duty. This is where the large majority of industrial AI sits, and saying so plainly is useful: the Act is not a blanket burden on engineering use of machine learning.

Separately: general-purpose AI models

Providers of general-purpose models (the frontier labs) have their own obligations, applicable since 2 August 2025: technical documentation, a copyright policy, a public summary of training data, and further requirements for models presenting systemic risk. You are almost certainly not a GPAI provider — but you should ask your vendors for their documentation, because you will need it for your own assessments.

10.3 · The timeline, as amended

The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. In 2026 the high-risk deadlines were deferred by the Digital AI Omnibus — a genuinely significant change, and a good illustration of why you should not build a compliance plan from a slide deck older than a few months.

Application dates

As of September 2026

The Digital AI Omnibus on AI was proposed by the Commission on 19 November 2025, agreed and signed in July 2026, and entered into force on 27 July 2026. It deferred the Annex III high-risk deadline from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 and the Annex I deadline to 2 August 2028, while leaving the Article 50 transparency obligations on their original schedule. It also made other targeted changes — confirm the consolidated text with your legal function rather than relying on this summary.

What the deferral does and does not mean

It buys time for the heaviest obligations. It does not defer the prohibitions (in force since February 2025), the AI-literacy duty (same date), the GPAI obligations (August 2025), or the transparency rules (August 2026). And for anything that will be high-risk, the extra time is only useful if it is spent building the documentation and evaluation practice that the obligations require — which, conveniently, is modules 7 and 8.

Provider or deployer — the distinction that decides your workload

Provider

You develop an AI system and place it on the market or put it into service under your own name — or you put your name on someone else's, or you substantially modify one, or you change its intended purpose into a high-risk one.

For a high-risk system that means: risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, automatic logging, instructions for deployers, human-oversight design, accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity requirements, a quality management system, conformity assessment, CE marking, registration, post-market monitoring and serious-incident reporting.

Deployer

You use an AI system under your own authority, in the course of your professional activity. This is what most companies are, most of the time.

For a high-risk system that means: use it according to the provider's instructions, assign competent human oversight with the authority to override, ensure input data is relevant and representative, monitor operation and report serious incidents, keep the logs, and inform affected workers and their representatives before putting it into use. Lighter than provider duties — and not nothing.

The trap

Fine-tuning a vendor's model on your data, or wrapping it in your own product and putting your name on it, can move you from deployer to provider — and with it the entire documentation and conformity burden. Establish which side of that line each project is on before the architecture is fixed, because it is a far cheaper conversation then.

Shadow AI is the actual exposure

10.6 · Attacks on the model itself

Section 10.5 was about what you send to a model. This section is about what someone else can do to it. A machine-learning system has attack surface that conventional IT security does not look at, because nothing is broken into: the attacker edits the training data, ships you a weights file, asks your API a lot of questions, or simply shows the model a carefully chosen input. Each of these works on a system that is fully patched and correctly configured.

Already covered: prompt injection

The language-model version of “a crafted input makes the model misbehave” is prompt injection — untrusted text that the model treats as instructions. Module 9.4 covers it, including why it is unsolved and how to design around it. Everything below applies to any trained model: a vision classifier, a regression surrogate or an LLM.

Adversarial examples

A small, deliberately chosen change to an input that flips the model's output while a human sees no meaningful difference. It is not random noise: the attacker uses the model's own gradient to find the direction in which the output is most sensitive, and pushes every input value a tiny step that way. In a high-dimensional input such as a camera image, thousands of tiny pushes add up to a large change in the output. Physical versions exist: researchers have made stop signs read as speed-limit signs with a few printed stickers, and steered a production lane-keeping system with small marks on the road surface.

Who realistically does itMostly researchers and red teams today. In the wild: people evading spam, malware and content filters. For a vehicle, a motivated individual with time and access to the car or the model.
What it costs youA misclassification at the moment it matters — for perception, that is a safety question, not only a security one — and a very quotable headline.
Practical defenceNever let one model be the only barrier: plausibility checks, sensor fusion, map priors. Test with attack tools as part of verification. Adversarial training helps against the attack you trained for, at a price in accuracy and compute (try it below).

Data poisoning and backdoors

The attacker changes the training data rather than the input. The crude version — mislabel a lot of data — degrades accuracy and gets noticed. The dangerous version is a backdoor: a small number of training examples carry a trigger pattern (a sticker, a pixel patch, a phrase, an odd value in an unused field) and the attacker's chosen label. The model learns two things: the real task, and “trigger means this answer”. Because your test set contains no triggers, every evaluation in module 8 comes back clean.

Who realistically does itAn insider; a compromised annotation or data supplier; anyone who can write to a source you scrape. Researchers showed that for about $60 of expired domains they could have controlled a small fraction of two large public image datasets.
What it costs youA model that is wrong exactly when the attacker wants, and passes every test you own. When you inherit a pre-trained model, you inherit its training data and whatever was in it.
Practical defenceProvenance and access control on training data, versioned and hashed (module 7). A small, independently verified data set. Label-consistency checks (try one below). Prefer models whose training data you can name.

Model extraction, membership inference and data leakage

Model extraction (“stealing”)

Anyone who can query a model can train a copy of it on its answers. Full probability outputs make it cheaper; enough queries make it work regardless. The copy is also a rehearsal space: adversarial examples crafted on it often transfer to the original.

WhoCompetitors, and anyone with API access and a reason.
CostThe training spend and the know-how in the model, and an attacker who can now craft attacks offline.
DefenceAuthentication, per-client query budgets and rate limits, monitoring for systematic sweeps, returning labels or rounded scores rather than full probabilities, contract terms. Assume an exposed model can be approximated; decide what that is worth.

Membership inference and training-data leakage

Models behave measurably differently on data they were trained on — more confident, lower loss — so an attacker can test whether a given record was in the training set. Large models go further and can reproduce training text verbatim: researchers have extracted names, addresses and code from deployed language models with ordinary queries.

WhoResearchers, curious users, litigants — and colleagues who should not see the data.
CostA personal-data breach under GDPR, or trade secrets coming back out of a model fine-tuned on internal documents to anyone who can use it.
DefenceDo not train on what you cannot afford to leak. Give the model the same access control as its training data. Overfitted models leak more, so regularise and deduplicate. Prefer permission-aware retrieval over fine-tuning on confidential documents (section 10.5).

Supply chain: weights, datasets and packages you download

Most teams start from a downloaded model and a downloaded data set. A model file is not inert data. The widespread Python pickle format — behind many .pt, .bin, .pkl and joblib files — executes code when it is loaded, and security researchers have found models on public hubs that open a reverse shell on whoever loads them. Loading options such as trust_remote_code=True run code from the repository by design. And the weights themselves may carry a backdoor from someone else's poisoned data.

Who realistically does itOpportunistic attackers uploading to public hubs and package indexes, including typosquatted names. This one is not theoretical and does not need a skilled adversary.
What it costs youArbitrary code execution on a developer workstation or training server — with access to everything that machine can reach.
Practical defencePrefer safetensors or ONNX over pickle; load with weights_only=True; never trust_remote_code without reading the code. An internal model registry with approval, pinned hashes and a scan. Record what you used, as you would a software bill of materials, and review the licence (section 10.7).
Where this belongs in your process

For a high-risk system, Article 15 of the AI Act names these attacks explicitly: providers must address data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples and confidentiality attacks where appropriate. For anything in a vehicle, the natural home is the cybersecurity process you already run — the threat analysis under ISO/SAE 21434 and UN R155 — with the adversarial-input cases also considered in the safety argument. Add the model, its training data and its weights file to the asset list. That one step gets most of this section onto someone's desk.

Keep it proportionate

For most internal industrial machine learning — a defect classifier on a closed line, a simulation surrogate — the realistic threats are the boring ones: a malicious file from a public hub, and confidential data leaking out of a model shared too widely. Get those two right first. Adversarial robustness becomes a first-order engineering requirement when the model sees inputs an outsider can influence, and it is critical when that model is part of a safety function.

  • Run the first lab with “smallest ε” on three or four images. The point to land: the perturbed image is still a left arrow to everyone in the room, just a slightly noisier one. Be honest that at 12×12 the change is visible; that is the price of a toy you can compute live. Then switch to random noise at the same ε and show it does nothing. The attack works because it is aimed, not because it is large.
  • Explain why size matters: for a linear model the change in score is ε times the sum of the absolute weights (a little less where pixels clip at 0 or 1) — many tiny changes, all aimed the same way — and in high dimension there are many more of them to add up. On 144 pixels it takes ε ≈ 0.1. On a real camera image with hundreds of thousands of values, a few grey levels out of 255 is typically enough.
  • In the backdoor lab, note that clean accuracy never moves. That is the whole problem: no test you already have can detect it, which is why the defences are about provenance, not evaluation. Then tick the defence and say what it does not catch: a “clean-label” attack whose poisoned images are correctly labelled would get straight through.
  • If the room is from IT security, the supply-chain card is the one they will recognise. Ask whether the model registry is in scope of their existing software-supply-chain controls. Usually it is not.

10.7 · Intellectual property, briefly and carefully

Inputs — what models were trained on

Multiple major lawsuits over the use of copyrighted material in training are unresolved across several jurisdictions, and outcomes differ. The EU has a text-and-data-mining framework with a rights-holder opt-out, and the AI Act requires GPAI providers to have a copyright policy and publish a training-data summary.

What this means for you as a user: prefer vendors who offer an IP indemnity, and read what it actually covers. Several major vendors now do.

Outputs — who owns what comes out

Broadly, copyright protects human creative expression. Purely machine-generated output may attract no copyright at all in several jurisdictions, including under EU and US practice — which means you may not be able to stop anyone else using it. Meaningful human authorship changes the analysis.

Practical consequence: do not assume AI-generated material is yours to protect. For anything that matters commercially — a design, a brand asset, code in a product — involve legal and document the human contribution.

“Open-weight” is not “open source”

Several widely used open-weight licences impose acceptable-use restrictions, user-count thresholds, naming requirements or limits on using outputs to train competing models. Have the licence reviewed before you build a product on the model. The word “open” is doing marketing work in this sentence.

Code assistants

A model can reproduce training code, including copyleft-licensed code, which creates a licence-contamination question in a commercial product. Mitigations: vendor settings that suppress verbatim matches, licence scanning in CI, and keeping generated code out of anything you ship without review. Treat this as a supply-chain control, not a legal footnote. Module 12.7 covers working with these tools day to day.

Trade secrets — the one people forget

Trade-secret protection depends on having taken reasonable steps to keep the information secret. Pasting an unreleased design into a third-party service whose terms permit training on your input can undermine that argument. This risk is independent of data protection, it applies to information about no identifiable person at all, and it is frequently the more serious exposure for an engineering organisation.

10.9 · An operating model that is not theatre

The four things that do the work

  1. An AI inventory. One list of every AI use in the company, with owner, purpose, data touched, tier and review date. You cannot govern what you cannot enumerate, and building this list is usually the most revealing week of the whole programme.
  2. One lightweight review gate proportionate to tier: minimal risk gets a register entry, high-risk gets a full assessment. A single heavy process for everything guarantees people route around it.
  3. A sanctioned toolset that is genuinely good, so the compliant path is also the easy path.
  4. AI literacy — which the Act requires of providers and deployers alike, and which this seminar series is. Record attendance: it is evidence.

The four that are theatre

  • A 40-page policy nobody reads, with no approved tool provided.
  • A committee that reviews slides rather than systems, after the build.
  • A blanket ban, which produces shadow AI and no visibility.
  • A vendor questionnaire returned by the vendor's marketing department and filed unread.

The test for any control: does it change what gets built, or only what gets documented?
