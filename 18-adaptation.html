Module 18 · Part IV — Shipping it

Adapting models: fine-tuning, LoRA, distillation

Module 4 asked whether to prompt, retrieve, fine-tune or train, and showed retrieval working. This module finishes the answer: what each option actually changes inside the model, what it costs, what it breaks, and how to choose. Four small networks train in your browser while you read.

Cost figures indicative, September 2026 — verify before each delivery

The one-sentence version

Prompting and retrieval change what the model is shown; fine-tuning, LoRA and distillation change the weights. Put knowledge in the input, put behaviour in the weights — and change the weights only when you have an evaluation set that can prove it helped.

18.1 · The adaptation ladder

There are five ways to make a general model do your job. Each rung costs roughly ten times more than the one below it in money, time or data, and each rung changes something different. The mistake that wastes the most money in corporate AI is climbing a rung that changes the wrong thing.

Not a rung: distillation (18.5) does not add capability. It copies a large model's behaviour into a smaller, cheaper one. It answers a different question — “how do we afford this at volume?” — and is how most of the small, capable models you can buy were made.

Knowledge belongs outside the weights

Facts, documents, anything that changes

Part numbers, torque values, last month's decision. In the input they can be cited, updated in seconds, restricted per user and deleted. In the weights they are recalled unreliably, cannot be traced to a source, go stale at the next revision and cannot be removed without retraining. Module 4.8 showed the input route working.

Behaviour belongs inside them

How to answer, not what is true

Always emit valid JSON in your schema. Classify a defect report into one of 40 codes the way your quality team does. Write in the register of your test reports. These are hard to describe in a prompt and easy to show with examples — which is exactly what fine-tuning consumes.

  • About 6 minutes. Open by recalling the 4.8 table: “last time we stopped at the question. Today we answer it, and we train the networks to prove it.”
  • The knowledge/behaviour split is the one idea to land. Everything else in the module is a consequence of it.
  • Expected question: “a vendor says their fine-tuning service will teach the model our documentation.” Answer: it will teach it to sound like your documentation. Ask them to show the recall rate on held-out factual questions, with citations, and compare it with RAG on the same questions.

18.2 · Transfer learning: why fine-tuning works at all

A network trained on one job has spent most of its effort learning features — which inputs matter and which combinations of them are useful. If a second job shares those features, you do not need to learn them again. You start from the trained weights and adjust them with a small amount of new data. That is all fine-tuning is, and it is why a model pretrained on the internet can learn your task from a thousand examples rather than a billion.

The lab makes this concrete with a situation every engineer here recognises: plenty of simulation data, very few test-bench measurements. The network has six input channels, of which only two matter (it is not told which). The simulator gives 800 labelled points. The bench — the truth — has a boundary that is offset from the simulator's by a sim-to-real gap, and every bench point is expensive. Compare a network trained only on the bench points with one pretrained on simulation and then fine-tuned on the same bench points.

The boundary shapes are invented for the exercise — a wave in two channels and an offset for the gap — and are not a model of any real engine. “Top layers only” freezes the first layer, which is the part that has learnt which channels matter.

What the sweep shows

Pretraining matters most when target data is scarce. With a handful of bench points the fine-tuned network is far ahead; by a hundred or so the gap has narrowed to a few points. Transfer buys data efficiency, not a higher ceiling. If you have plenty of target data, the case for starting from a pretrained model is speed, not accuracy.

What actually transfers

Push the gap to 2. The simulator model on its own is now poor, yet fine-tuning still helps — because what transfers is the features (which channels matter), not the boundary. Transfer fails when the features themselves differ: if the bench depended on a channel the simulator ignored, pretraining would teach the network to ignore it too. How much two jobs share is a claim to test, not assume.

Why freezing the first layer helps with few examples

With eight bench points and six input channels, there are many ways to separate the points perfectly, most of them nonsense — for example by leaning on a nuisance channel that happens to correlate in this sample. A network trained from scratch has no reason to prefer the sensible solution. The pretrained first layer has already learnt, from 800 simulator points, to ignore the four channels that do not matter. Freezing it removes the nonsense options entirely.

Letting every layer move gives the eight points the power to overwrite what was learnt from the 800. Try “All layers” at eight points: the result is usually worse. The large-model versions of this choice are LoRA (18.4), adapters and “freeze the backbone, train the head” — all ways of limiting how much a small dataset is allowed to change.

  • About 8 minutes. Pretraining runs automatically when the page loads (about two seconds). The lab opens at 8 bench points. Drag down to 4 — the scratch map falls apart while the fine-tuned one keeps the wave shape — then up to 64, where the two look alike.
  • Press New bench sample two or three times at 8 points. The scratch result swings wildly between samples. That variance is the real cost of small data, and it is what pretraining reduces.
  • Run the sweep while you talk. Typical result at gap 1: about 70% from scratch against about 83% fine-tuned at 4 points, about 80% against 87% at 8, narrowing to a few points by 64–128. Exact figures vary with the sample; that is honest, not a bug. Some samples show no gain at all — when the simulator model already classifies every bench point correctly, there is nothing for the fine-tune to learn from.
  • Expected question: “so can we pretrain on simulation and fine-tune on test data?” Yes — it is a well-established pattern. The catch is the gap: if the simulator is wrong in a structured way, the network inherits the error wherever the bench data is thin. Hold out bench data to measure it.

18.3 · Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning optimises for the new data and nothing else. Gradient descent has no notion of “and please keep doing what you did before”. Weights that encoded the old behaviour get repurposed, and performance on the old job collapses — even where the new data says nothing at all about it. The name is from 1989 and it is still the main practical risk of fine-tuning.

In the lab, a network has learnt one rule across the whole operating range. A new variant behaves differently only in the high-load corner (shaded), and we have 60 labelled points there. A network could easily represent both — the old rule outside the corner, the new rule inside. Fine-tune on the corner points alone and watch what happens to the rest of the map.

The faint dashed curves on the accuracy chart are your previous run, for comparison. The rules themselves are invented for the exercise.

In a language model

Fine-tune a general model on thousands of defect reports and it may get better at defect reports and worse at everything else: general reasoning, other languages, following instructions it used to follow. Published work has also shown that fine-tuning on entirely benign data can weaken a model's safety behaviour. Nobody sees this unless they test for it.

What reduces it

Replay — keep mixing in general data. Less movement — lower learning rate, fewer steps, freeze layers. Separate weights — LoRA leaves the original untouched, so unloading the adapter restores the original exactly. None of these is free, and none removes the need to measure.

What detects it

A regression suite: the evaluation set from module 8 for the new task, plus a set for the things the model already did that you rely on. Run both before and after every fine-tune. If you only measure the new task, the dashboard will show an improvement while the product gets worse.

  • About 6 minutes. Run it first at 0% replay and learning rate 0.1. The new corner is learnt within a few dozen steps; the old job slides from about 99% towards 60% as training continues. Then set replay to 10% and run again — the old job barely moves. The previous run stays on the chart as a faint line.
  • Then lower the learning rate to 0.03 with no replay: the forgetting is slower, not absent. That is the trap — a short evaluation after a cautious fine-tune can look fine.
  • Expected question: “why does it forget a region it was never trained on?” Because the weights are shared across the whole input space; nothing in the loss protects the old region, so the cheapest way to fit the corner moves boundaries everywhere.

18.4 · LoRA: fine-tuning a small slice of the weights

Full fine-tuning of an 8-billion-parameter model updates 8 billion numbers, and training needs memory for the gradients and optimiser state as well — roughly 16 bytes per parameter, about 128 GB, so several large GPUs. LoRA (low-rank adaptation, 2021) freezes the original weight matrix W₀ and learns only a correction, written as the product of two thin matrices.

W = W₀ + B · A     B is d × r, A is r × k A full update of a d × k matrix has d·k numbers. The LoRA update has r·(d + k). For a 4096 × 4096 layer at r = 8 that is 65,536 instead of 16.8 million.

The product B·A can only represent a matrix of rank r or less — a sum of r “directions” of change. LoRA works because the updates fine-tuning actually needs turn out, empirically, to be close to low rank: most of the change lives in a few directions. The lab lets you check what “close to low rank” means. Pick a target update, pick r, and the best possible rank-r approximation is computed by singular value decomposition (the Eckart–Young result guarantees nothing of rank r does better).

Lab computed live: ΔW is decomposed by one-sided Jacobi SVD in JavaScript; the gradient-descent fit minimises ‖B·A − ΔW‖² directly. The “structured” target is synthetic — random directions with singular values that decay at the rate you set — standing in for a real fine-tuning update, which we cannot ship in a web page. “Real: from lab 18.2” uses the actual change the fine-tune made to that network's 12 × 12 middle layer (run lab 18.2 with “All layers” or “Top layers” first). Real-scale figures assume an 8B model with 32 layers and LoRA on four 4096 × 4096 attention projections per layer, stored at 16 bits; real architectures differ in detail.

If you load the real update, notice how little rank it needs. That is structural, not luck: with the first layer frozen, every gradient step for the middle layer is built from the activation patterns of the bench points, so the whole update has rank no higher than the number of bench points. Fine-tuning on a narrow dataset produces narrow updates — the intuition behind LoRA.

What LoRA buys you operationally
  • Training fits on one GPU, and with a 4-bit quantised base (QLoRA) even a large model fits on one card.
  • The adapter is a file of tens of megabytes. One base model can serve many adapters — one per task or department — swapped per request.
  • Rollback is trivial. Unload the adapter and you have the original model back, bit for bit.
What it does not change
  • It is still fine-tuning. Same data requirements, same governance questions, same forgetting risk (smaller, not zero).
  • An adapter belongs to one exact base model. When the base is replaced, the adapter is useless; you retrain and re-evaluate.
  • Rank is a hyperparameter, not a guarantee. Some tasks need more than r = 8. You find out by measuring.
  • About 8 minutes. Start on “Structured”, r = 1, then step up to 4 and 8: the captured share climbs past 90% while the parameter count stays a fraction of the full matrix. Then switch to “Random noise”: now rank 8 captures only about half, and you need most of the 32 directions. LoRA's premise is that real updates look like the first case, not the second.
  • Press “Fit B·A by gradient descent” — the error curve drops onto the dashed SVD optimum. That is the point: LoRA's training finds the same best low-rank answer the maths says exists.
  • Expected question: “what rank should we use?” Start at 8 or 16, compare against a higher rank on your evaluation set, and stop increasing when the score stops moving. The LoRA paper reported very low ranks working for some tasks on GPT-3; that is evidence, not a rule.

18.5 · Distillation: a big model teaching a small one

A large model is expensive to run on every request. Distillation trains a much smaller student to imitate a large teacher. Two things make it work better than training the student on the original labels. First, the teacher can label as many unlabelled inputs as you can afford to send it — your own logs, documents, sensor records. Second, the teacher gives soft labels: not just “class B” but “72% class B”, which says how close to the boundary each example sits. This is how most of the small, cheap, capable models of the last two years were made (the distillation card in module 5).

Computed live. The teacher is a 2-32-32-1 network trained on 4,000 labelled points of an invented spiral-shaped rule (5% of labels flipped, as real labels are noisy). Each student is a 2-h-h-1 network for h = 2 … 12, trained for 2,500 steps on the teacher's labels for the transfer set, two samples per size; accuracy is on 1,500 clean held-out points. Hard labels = the teacher's yes/no; soft labels = its probability, softened by the temperature. Students in each pair share their starting weights and batches, so the comparison is like-for-like.

What to read from it

Small students hit a capacity wall that no labelling scheme removes — two units per layer cannot draw a spiral. Students with 6–8 units per layer get within a few points of the teacher at about a tenth of its size, if they learn from soft labels. Each soft label says how close to the boundary a point is, which a yes/no cannot; and a smooth target is easier for a small network to fit in the same number of steps. Both effects are part of the gain you see. In a language model the teacher's probabilities over a 100,000-token vocabulary also say which wrong answers were nearly right — far more information than this two-class toy can carry.

Before you distil from an API
  • Read the terms. Many commercial model licences restrict using outputs to train other models.
  • The student inherits the teacher's errors, confidently, and adds its own. Evaluate it against ground truth, not against the teacher.
  • It is a volume play. The saving is per request; it repays the build only when the request count is large.
  • About 7 minutes. The teacher trains on page load and the students distil automatically; releasing a slider re-runs them (about two seconds). At the default 316 inputs, the soft-label curve typically sits five to ten points above the hard-label one for 4–8 units per layer, and the 8-unit soft student lands within a few points of the teacher. At ~3,000 inputs the gap narrows but usually does not close. With two samples per size there is noise — point at the faint dots, not just the line.
  • Then raise the temperature to about 2.5: the smallest students improve noticeably, because the target they are asked to match becomes smoother. Do not oversell temperature beyond that; its classic justification is many-class outputs.
  • Expected question: “is this what DeepSeek / the small open models did?” Broadly yes — large models generating training data and soft targets for smaller ones is standard practice. Details of any specific vendor's pipeline are usually not public.

18.7 · When fine-tuning is the wrong answer

Fine-tuning is real and useful. It is also the most over-requested technique in corporate AI, usually because it sounds like “making the model ours”. These are the situations where it is the wrong tool, roughly in order of how often they occur.

Wrong tool

To add facts

Fine-tuning on documents teaches the model to produce text that sounds like your documents. It does not reliably make it recall the right value for the right question — and when it half-remembers, it answers fluently and wrongly, with nothing to cite. Studies comparing the two have found retrieval clearly better for injecting knowledge, and that fine-tuning on facts the model did not already know can increase its tendency to make things up.

Instead: RAG (module 4.8). Facts stay in documents you control, with citations, permissions and instant updates.

Wrong time

When the base model will be replaced in six months

An adapter or fine-tune is welded to one exact base model. Base models are currently superseded every few months, and the new one usually beats your fine-tuned old one out of the box. Every replacement means re-curating, retraining, re-evaluating and re-approving — or staying on an ageing model.

Instead: invest in what carries over — prompts, retrieval, the evaluation set and the guard-rails. Fine-tune when the behaviour is stable and the gain survives a fresh base.

Premature

Before prompting has been tried seriously

Module 12's techniques — clear instructions, worked examples, a fixed output schema — solve most format and style problems in an afternoon. If nobody can show the prompt that was tried and the evaluation score it achieved, the fine-tuning project has no baseline and cannot prove it helped.

Unmeasurable

Without an evaluation set

If you cannot score the current system on a few hundred held-out cases (module 8), you cannot tell whether fine-tuning improved it, and you cannot detect the forgetting from 18.3. Build the evaluation first. It is also the asset that lets you switch base models safely.

Ungovernable

When the data cannot live in weights

Training data becomes part of the model. Personal data cannot be deleted record-by-record afterwards; confidential supplier IP under NDA may not be licensed for it; export controlled data makes the weights export controlled. A model fine-tuned on secret data is a secret artefact. And modifying a model can make you its provider under the AI Act (module 10).

Misdiagnosed

When the real problem is retrieval

A RAG system that answers badly is most often retrieving the wrong passages. Fine-tuning the generator does not fix the search. Measure retrieval on its own first: for each test question, was the right passage in the top five?

When it is the right answer

A stable, narrow, high-volume task; hundreds to thousands of consistent examples; an evaluation set that shows prompting has plateaued; and data you are allowed to put into weights. Typical winners: classifying reports into fixed codes, extracting fields into a strict schema, matching a house style, or replacing a large model with a small fine-tuned one that is cheaper, faster and can run on your own hardware.

  • About 4 minutes. The first two cards are the brief; the rest are a checklist people can take to a project review.
  • Expected pushback: “but the vendor offers fine-tuning on our documents.” Ask for the evaluation: factual accuracy on held-out questions, with and without the fine-tune, against RAG on the same questions. If they cannot produce it, nobody has measured it.

Where we go next

What to take back to your desk

  1. Name what has to change. Knowledge goes in the input; behaviour goes in the weights.
  2. Climb the ladder one rung at a time, and only with an evaluation score showing the rung below has plateaued.
  3. Fine-tune with a regression suite, not just a target-task score — forgetting is silent.
  4. Prefer LoRA-style adapters when you do fine-tune: cheap, small, reversible.
  5. Count the base-model replacements. Every one resets a fine-tune and leaves prompts, retrieval and evaluations standing.
