Module 13 · Part III — Applying it

Human factors and adoption

Most AI pilots that fail do not fail technically. They fail because of how people used, ignored or over-trusted them. This module is about the part of the system that is made of people — and it starts by demonstrating the problem on you.

Ten decisions. In each one you have the data and a recommendation from an AI system that has been running for six months with a good track record. Accept or override. Everything you need to judge each case is on the screen.

13.2 · Trust calibration

There are two ways to get this wrong and only a narrow band of getting it right. Over-trust and under-trust are both expensive, and organisations usually swing from one to the other rather than landing in between.

Over-trust — automation bias

The documented tendency to accept an automated recommendation without the scrutiny you would apply to a colleague's. It is worse when the system is usually right, when the human is busy, when accountability is diffuse, and when the interface presents a confident single answer rather than options.

The cruel part: a system that is right 99% of the time produces more automation bias than one that is right 80% of the time, because there is no recent error to keep you sceptical. Reliability creates complacency.

Under-trust — algorithm aversion

People abandon a system after seeing it make one error, even when it is demonstrably better than the alternative. The same people forgive human colleagues the same mistake routinely, and forgive their own more readily still.

The practical consequence: a tool that is better on average but occasionally visibly wrong gets switched off, and the measurably worse manual process continues. Expect this, and set expectations about the error rate before go-live rather than after the first incident.

A simple model, deliberately: each case is either accepted blindly or reviewed; a reviewer catches a model error with the stated probability and then applies their own (imperfect) judgement, and occasionally overrides a correct answer. The shape of the curve is the point, not the exact numbers.

The result worth arguing about

For most realistic settings the best outcome is at neither end. Total reliance throws away the human's ability to catch errors; total review throws away the speed that justified the system and, worse, degrades into rubber-stamping because nobody can review 400 items a day attentively. The design question is not “do we trust it” but “which cases get reviewed, and by whom” — and the answer should come from the calibration and cost work in module 8, not from a policy sentence.

13.3 · How to review output you cannot fully verify

The honest difficulty: checking a long AI-written document properly can take as long as writing it, which destroys the benefit. These are the strategies that actually work, by task type.

Verify the structure, not the prose

For extraction and tabulation, check a random sample of rows against the source — five rows takes a minute and gives you a real error rate. Never read the whole table; sample it, and record the number. That number is the beginning of module 8.

Demand quotations

Make every factual claim carry the sentence it came from and a clause reference. You are then checking a citation, not evaluating an argument — an order of magnitude faster, and it catches the failure mode that matters most.

Check the load-bearing claim only

In an analysis, identify the one or two facts the conclusion actually rests on and verify those. The rest is context and a small error there is cheap. This is triage, and it is how experienced reviewers read anything.

Recompute the numbers

Never accept arithmetic from a language model (module 4.11). If a number matters, recompute it in a spreadsheet or make the model use a calculator and show the call.

Ask for the assumptions

“List everything you had to assume” converts hidden guesses into a short, reviewable list. It is the single highest-leverage sentence to add to any drafting prompt.

Review the inputs instead

Sometimes the efficient check is upstream: did it retrieve the right documents? If the sources are right and the prompt forbids outside knowledge, the output is far more likely to be right — and the retrieval is much easier to inspect.

The anti-pattern

“A human reviews every output” written into a process document, with no time allocated and no guidance on what to look at. Within three weeks it becomes a click. An unfunded review step is not a control — it is an alibi, and it is worse than no review because everyone believes it is happening.

13.4 · Where the human sits

Human in the loop

The system proposes; a person decides and acts. Nothing happens without a human action.

Use for: anything consequential, irreversible, external, safety-relevant or regulated. Also the right starting point for everything, because it generates the data that tells you whether more autonomy is justified.

Cost: you keep the human time, so the benefit is quality and speed of drafting rather than headcount.

Human on the loop

The system acts; a person monitors, samples and can intervene or roll back.

Use for: high volume, low individual consequence, reversible actions, with good logging and a real kill switch (module 9.8).

Cost: you need genuine monitoring — sampled audits with recorded error rates, not a dashboard nobody opens. Specify the sampling rate and who does it.

Human out of the loop

Fully automatic, reviewed only in aggregate.

Use for: narrow, well-measured, low-stakes, high-volume tasks where the error rate is known and the cost of an error is bounded and acceptable.

Cost: the burden of proof. You should be able to state the measured error rate, the cost per error, and why that trade is acceptable — in writing.

The staging that works, again

Start in the loop. Measure how often the human agrees with the system, and on which kinds of case. After a few hundred decisions you will find a segment where agreement is near-total — that segment, and only that segment, is a candidate for moving to on-the-loop. This is the same argument as module 9.8, and it is the only route to autonomy that survives a serious review.

13.5 · Deskilling

A real and under-discussed cost. If the system does the task, people stop practising the task — and the expertise you need in order to supervise the system is the same expertise the system is eroding. Aviation has studied this for decades under the name of automation-induced skill decay, and the lessons transfer.

The specific risk

A junior engineer who has never sized a component by hand cannot tell when the tool's answer is absurd. The tool works 99% of the time, so the gap is invisible — until the 1%, which is exactly when judgement is needed.

What helps

  • Teach the fundamentals first, tools second — the reason this course starts with a perceptron and not with a prompt.
  • Periodic unaided work: estimate before you ask.
  • Rotate people through the manual process deliberately.
  • Keep and maintain the ability to run the old way.

What does not help

Banning the tool. People will use it anyway, less visibly and with less training. The objective is competent supervision, not abstinence — and competent supervision has to be built deliberately, because it no longer arrives as a by-product of doing the work.

13.6 · Why pilots fail

Six patterns. In my experience of how these programmes go, technical inadequacy is well down the list.

1 · It solved a problem nobody had

Built because the technology was interesting, not because someone was complaining. The tell: nobody can name the person whose week gets better. Fix: start from a complaint, not from a capability.

2 · It saved the wrong minutes

It automated the eight-minute step in a process whose real cost was a three-day approval wait. Genuine improvement, no measurable effect. Fix: map the end-to-end time before choosing where to intervene.

3 · It sat outside the tools people use

A separate portal requiring a separate login, while the work happens in the PLM system and Outlook. Adoption decays to the enthusiasts within a month. Fix: integrate into the existing workflow, or accept that you built a demo.

4 · The first visible error killed it

Algorithm aversion from 13.2, usually compounded by having promised near-perfection. Fix: state the expected error rate and the review strategy before go-live, so the first error confirms your description instead of contradicting it.

5 · Nobody was accountable for the outcome

Built by a central team, handed to a department that did not ask for it and owns no objective it serves. Fix: a named owner in the receiving function, with a target the tool actually moves, from before the first line of code.

6 · It made someone's job worse

The drafting got faster and the reviewing got longer, and the reviewer was not consulted. Quiet, effective resistance follows. Fix: ask whose work increases, and fix that before launch. If the answer is “the most senior person”, the design is wrong.

The diagnostic question

Before building anything, ask: “Whose week gets measurably better, by how much, and have they told us they want this?” If you cannot answer all three parts with a name and a number, you have a technology project rather than an improvement project. That is sometimes legitimate — as learning — but it should be labelled honestly, and it should not be measured as though it were the other thing.

13.7 · Measuring adoption without fooling yourself

Vanity metrics

  • Licences assigned. Measures procurement, not use.
  • Total queries. Rises with curiosity and with frustrated retries alike.
  • Users who tried it once. Everyone tries a new tool once.
  • Satisfaction scores in the first month. Measures novelty.
  • Time saved, self-reported. People are poor at estimating this and are motivated to please you.

Metrics that mean something

  • Weekly returning users as a share of those who tried it. Retention, not reach.
  • Task completion rate — of the things people started, how many finished usefully.
  • Override rate, and its trend. High and falling means learning; high and flat means the system is wrong; near zero means nobody is checking (13.1).
  • End-to-end cycle time for the real process, measured before and after.
  • Quality of the output of the process, judged the way it was always judged.
  • A qualitative one: would you be annoyed if we switched it off tomorrow?
The two numbers to report to a steering committee

Weekly returning users and end-to-end cycle time for the process. The first tells you whether the tool earned its place in people's habits; the second tells you whether it changed anything the business cares about. Everything else is supporting detail — and a programme that can only report licences and total queries after six months is telling you something it does not intend to.

  • Run the experiment in 13.1 live, with the room voting. Do not warn them. The number of people who accepted a recommendation contradicted by data on the same screen is the most useful statistic you will produce all series, and it is about them rather than about the technology.
  • Say clearly that automation bias is not a failure of intelligence or attention. It is a well-documented, universal response to reliable automation, and the answer is system design, not exhortation.
  • The reliance curve in 13.2 is worth drawing on a whiteboard for managers. “Neither always nor never” is a more useful policy position than either extreme, and it is much easier to defend once they have seen the shape.
  • If your audience includes people whose jobs are affected, address it directly and early. Evasion on this point costs you the room, and the honest version — what changes, what does not, what is still undecided — is generally received better than reassurance.
