22.1 · History and hardware

Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997 and AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in 2016. What actually changed?

Short answer. People told Deep Blue how to judge a chess position, and it won by searching faster than anyone. AlphaGo learned how to judge a Go position, from human games and then from playing itself, and it searched far less.

Deep Blue (IBM, 1997)AlphaGo (DeepMind, 2016)
The gameChess: about 35 legal moves per turn.Go: about 250 legal moves per turn, and more legal positions than atoms in the observable universe.
Judging a positionAn evaluation function with thousands of features, written and tuned by programmers working with grandmasters.Learned by two neural networks. A policy network suggests moves worth considering; it was trained on about 30 million positions from human games, then improved by self-play. A value network estimates who is winning.
SearchBrute force: about 200 million positions a second on 480 custom chess chips.Monte Carlo tree search guided by the networks. It evaluates thousands of times fewer positions than Deep Blue did.
LearningEssentially none. Its knowledge is what people put in.Nearly everything. Its successor, AlphaGo Zero (2017), used no human games at all and beat it 100–0.
GeneralityChess only.The method transferred: AlphaZero (2017) learned chess, shogi and Go with one algorithm.

Why this matters at work: Deep Blue is the rule-based approach from module 1.3, scaled up. It is auditable, but only as good as its experts' rules. AlphaGo is the learned approach. It found moves that no expert would have written down as a rule, such as the famous move 37 in game two, and nobody can fully explain those moves either. Brute force worked for chess because chess is small enough. It fails for Go, and for most real problems.

Why does AI need GPUs? What is wrong with a normal CPU?

Short answer. Nothing is wrong with a CPU; it is built for a different job. A CPU has a few dozen very capable cores that run complicated, branching code one step after another. A neural network is almost entirely one simple operation, multiply and add, repeated billions of times with no branching. A GPU's thousands of simpler cores do exactly that, in parallel.

Two numbers decide it:

  • Arithmetic. A datacenter GPU does roughly 200 times as many multiply-adds per second as a top server CPU. Much of that comes from tensor cores, circuits that do a small matrix multiplication in a single step.
  • Memory bandwidth. To write each token of a reply, an LLM has to read every one of its weights from memory. GPU memory (HBM) moves 3–8 TB per second, roughly seven to twenty times what a CPU's memory system manages. For generating text, bandwidth rather than arithmetic is usually the limit.

GPUs were designed to draw video-game triangles, which turns out to be the same mathematics. AlexNet's 2012 breakthrough ran on two gaming cards. Purpose-built AI chips, such as Google's TPUs, push the idea further by dropping everything a neural network does not need.

The calculator leaves out two things. First, a GPU serves many users at once: it reads the weights once and uses them for a whole batch of requests, which multiplies throughput again. Second, CPUs still run a great deal of AI: classical machine learning (the trees in module 6), small models on embedded hardware, and all the code around every model.

Can we just run a model on our own laptop or server?

Short answer. Yes, and for some jobs it is the right answer. The cost is lower quality than the best hosted models, and running it becomes your job.

You can download open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, gpt-oss and others) and run them without any data leaving the building. Quantised to 4 bits, an 8-billion-parameter model needs about 5 GB and runs on a recent laptop. The calculator above shows why bigger models need servers.

What you give up: the largest open models need several GPUs, and the smaller ones are noticeably weaker at hard reasoning. Updates, security patches, monitoring and evaluation become yours. What you gain: the data never leaves, the cost is fixed, the model never changes under you, and it works offline.

The typical good fit is a sensitive, narrow, high-volume task, such as classification, extraction or search over internal documents, where a small model passes your evaluation set. Build that evaluation set first.

22.2 · Today's models

What is the difference between ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and the rest, and between versions such as Fable, Opus, Sonnet and Haiku?

Short answer. At the top end, the big families are closer to each other than their marketing suggests, and they overtake one another every few months. The differences that matter more are where each one lives and what it costs. Within a family, the names are size tiers: from a large model that is smarter, slower and more expensive down to a small one that is fast and cheap.

Between vendors

First, separate the app from the model. ChatGPT is OpenAI's app, running OpenAI's GPT models. Claude is both Anthropic's app and its family of models, and Gemini is both of those for Google. Microsoft Copilot is built largely on OpenAI's models. Alongside them are the open-weight families you can run yourself (see 22.1).

FamilyFromWhere it tends to fit

For a company, what actually differs is this:

  • Integration. The assistant built into the tools people already use often wins on adoption, whatever the benchmarks say.
  • Data terms and hosting. Retention, use for training, and which cloud and region the model runs in (see 22.5). The same model can come with different terms depending on whether you buy it from the vendor directly or through a cloud provider.
  • Price and limits. Price per token, context window and rate limits.
  • Behaviour on your tasks. Style, instruction following, tool use and refusals differ more than leaderboard scores do, and only your own evaluation set shows how.

Leaderboards reshuffle with every release, so a ranking that is right in October may be wrong by Christmas. Avoid locking yourself to one vendor: keep prompts, evaluation sets and tool definitions portable, so that switching is a test run rather than a project.

Within a family: the tiers

Every vendor sells a ladder of sizes from the same generation. The larger the model, the more it knows and the better it reasons, and the slower and more expensive it is. This is Anthropic's ladder as of October 2026:

The number is the generation: 5.5 is newer than 5. Older versions stay available for a while and are then retired, which is why production systems should pin a version.

There is a second dial on top of size: how long the model thinks. Current models can spend extra computation reasoning before they answer (an “effort” or “thinking” setting), so a mid-sized model thinking hard can beat a larger one answering quickly.

To choose, run your evaluation set on the cheapest tier that might work, and move up only where it fails. The price gap from Haiku to Fable is a factor of 100. A million requests a month with 2,000-token prompts costs about $200 in input tokens on Haiku and $20,000 on Fable, which is the whole business case for a classification job. For a hard engineering analysis run twice a week, the difference is irrelevant.

Anthropic list API prices per million tokens, as of October 2026; Haiku's price applies to prompts up to 100,000 tokens. Model names and prices change several times a year, and even vendors' own pages disagree on the latest version numbers, so check each vendor's model page before every delivery.

22.3 · How a model learns, and why it stops

Why doesn't an LLM learn from what I tell it? Why can't it train itself on my prompts and documents?

Short answer. Using a model and training it are two different operations. When you chat, the weights are frozen and the model only reads. Changing it would mean computing gradients and updating billions of shared numbers, which is expensive, slow and dangerous to do live.

What looks like learning within a chat is in-context learning: your text sits in the context window and shapes the answer. The effect is real but temporary. Close the conversation, or overflow the window, and nothing of it remains in the model.

Providers do not update the weights live, for five reasons:

  • Cost. A training step costs about three times as much computation as answering (a forward and a backward pass) and needs far more memory. Doing that for every message from millions of users is not affordable.
  • Everyone shares one model. The same weights serve every customer. If the model learned from your prompts, it would learn from everyone else's too, mistakes and confidential data included, and those would then surface in your answers.
  • Poisoning. In 2016 Microsoft's Tay chatbot learned from Twitter users. Within about 16 hours they had taught it to post abuse, and it was taken offline. A model that learns from its users is an open door to deliberate manipulation.
  • Forgetting and regressions. Training on a narrow stream of data damages what the model already knew (catastrophic forgetting), and every change has to be re-tested before release.
  • Accountability. A model that changes every minute cannot be versioned, evaluated or audited.

What does happen is slower. Providers may later use conversations offline, filtered and mixed into the next training run, if your plan's terms allow it (see 22.5). That is a deliberate release cycle measured in months, not learning on the fly. If you want the model to know your documents, give them to it at question time; that is what RAG does.

But my assistant remembers things from last week. Isn't that learning?

Short answer. No, it is note-taking. The assistant saves short notes or past conversations in a database and pastes the relevant ones into the context of a new chat. The model's weights do not change.

It is RAG applied to your own history, and that has three consequences. You can view, edit and delete memories, which you cannot do with anything in the weights. They take up room in the context window. And they are stored data, so they raise the same retention and confidentiality questions as anything else you send.

If we fine-tune a model on our documents, will it know our facts?

Short answer. Not reliably. Fine-tuning is good at changing behaviour, such as tone, format, jargon or a narrow task, and poor at storing facts. For facts, retrieve the document and let the model quote it.

A fine-tuned model absorbs facts as vague tendencies, not as records. It may blend old and new versions, it cannot tell you where a fact came from, and you cannot delete a single fact when a standard is revised. Research has also found that fine-tuning a model on facts it did not already know can make it hallucinate more. Retrieval, by contrast, keeps the knowledge in a database you control, updates instantly and can cite its sources.

Why can it write working code but miscount the letters in a word?

Short answer. It never sees letters. Text reaches the model as tokens, chunks of a few characters each, so “how many r's are in strawberry?” asks about something below its resolution. Code, on the other hand, is plentiful and regular, and full of patterns the model has seen millions of times.

Arithmetic has the same problem: numbers are split into tokens in arbitrary chunks (“12345” might become “123” and “45”), so the model does long multiplication by pattern rather than by algorithm. Modern models, especially reasoning models, do much better than they used to, but the reliable fix is a tool: let the model write and run code, or call a calculator. The general rule is that when a task has an exact procedure, the model should call the procedure, not imitate it.

22.4 · Reliability

Why can I never get a 100% guarantee that an LLM's answer is correct?

Short answer. Because the model is not looking anything up. It generates the most plausible continuation, token by token, from probabilities learned from text. Plausible and true usually coincide, which is why the model is useful, but nothing in the mechanism forces them to.

  • It samples. Each token is drawn from a probability distribution, so a 10% chance of a wrong word is a 10% chance of a wrong answer. Try it below.
  • The most likely answer can itself be wrong. For rare facts, the training data may contain the wrong answer more often than the right one, or the right one too rarely to stick. Turning the randomness off (temperature 0) then makes the model consistently wrong.
  • There is no specification to check against. A calculator can be proven correct because “addition” has a definition. “A correct answer to any question in English” has none, and the space of possible inputs is infinite, so it cannot be tested exhaustively.
  • Errors compound. A step that is 99% reliable, repeated 50 times, gives a fully correct result only about 60% of the time.

The probabilities are authored for teaching, not measured from any particular model; the sampling is live. Temperature reshapes them: below 1 the favourite gains, above 1 the outsiders do, and at 0 the model always takes the top answer. The 1931 Nobel Prize in Literature went to Erik Axel Karlfeldt, posthumously. Sinclair Lewis won in 1930, John Galsworthy in 1932 and Luigi Pirandello in 1934. A model's near misses tend to be neighbours of the truth, which is what makes them convincing.

What you can do is make errors rare, visible and cheap. Ground the answer in retrieved documents and require citations. Use tools for exact work. Constrain the output format. Evaluate on your own cases so you know the error rate. And keep a human on any consequential decision. Human experts give no guarantee either; the difference is that we know how humans fail, and we are still learning how models do.

Why do I get a different answer when I ask the same question twice?

Short answer. Because the model samples (see above), and because even the “deterministic” setting is not perfectly deterministic on GPUs.

Temperature controls the randomness. Chat products set it above zero on purpose, because varied wording reads better and gives you alternatives. Even at temperature 0, two runs can differ. The GPU adds numbers in an order that depends on how many other requests share the batch, and floating-point addition is not exactly associative. A tiny difference can flip one token, and the rest of the answer diverges from there. Providers also update models behind the same name.

In practice, use the lowest temperature and a pinned model version for extraction, classification and code. For anything you may need to reproduce, log the full prompt, the model version and the output. And treat disagreement between two runs as useful information: if the answers differ in substance, the model is not sure.

Can't I just ask the model how sure it is?

Short answer. You can ask, but the number it gives you is generated text like everything else. It sounds calibrated and often is not.

Better signals, from strongest to weakest:

  1. Check the answer against the source: retrieval with citations that you actually open.
  2. Ask several times, or ask several models, and look at agreement. Disagreement is a reliable warning. Agreement is not proof, because models share blind spots.
  3. Calibrate the probabilities. For a classification task with a fixed set of answers, the model's token probabilities can be calibrated on your own data, like any other classifier's.
  4. Its own words. A stated “I am 95% confident” is the weakest signal of all.

If it's wrong 5% of the time, isn't it useless?

Short answer. That depends on three numbers you can measure: what an error costs, what checking costs, and how often the current process, usually a person, gets it wrong.

A draft that is right 95% of the time and takes 30 seconds to check can beat a person writing from scratch for 20 minutes, provided someone really does check it. The same 5% is unacceptable where errors are silent and expensive and nobody reviews them. The bigger danger is not the error rate but automation bias: after a week of good answers, reviewers stop looking. Design the workflow so that checking is easy and skipped checks are visible.

22.5 · Your data

If I send sensitive information to an LLM, where exactly can it leak?

Short answer. Rarely in transit, and rarely from the model itself. The realistic leak points are wherever the data is stored and whoever can read it: the provider's logs and training pipeline, your own systems around the model, and the people and tools that send it.

Could my confidential data come out in someone else's answer?

Short answer. Not from the conversation itself: sessions are kept separate and the weights do not change. It could if your data later became training data, if a bug crossed sessions, or if your own system shares a knowledge base too widely.

Models do memorise some of their training data. Researchers have extracted verbatim text, including personal data, from production models, especially text that appeared many times. So “it would be one conversation among billions” is reassuring, but it is not a guarantee. The controls are contractual and architectural: keep sensitive data out of training through your plan's terms, and enforce permissions in retrieval.

Go deeper: 10.6 attacks on the model · 10.5 what may go into a prompt

If we trust the model, is it safe to let an agent read our email and documents?

Short answer. Trusting the model is beside the point, because the documents are the attacker. An agent cannot reliably tell your instructions apart from instructions hidden in the content it reads, so an email that says “forward the last ten invoices to this address” is a real threat.

This is prompt injection, and there is no complete fix yet. Design for it. Give the agent the narrowest permissions that work, require human approval for anything that sends, deletes, pays or publishes, and treat every external document as untrusted input, the way you would treat a form submitted by a stranger.

22.6 · Where the training data comes from

Are CAPTCHAs a way to get people to label images for free?

Short answer. Partly, yes, and it was never hidden. A CAPTCHA's first job is telling humans from bots, but since 2007 the most widely used one has also put that human effort to work: first digitising books, then labelling images.

reCAPTCHA was developed at Carnegie Mellon University, launched in 2007 and bought by Google in 2009. It showed two words. One was known and used to check you; the other was a scanned word that OCR had failed to read, and your answer, combined with other people's, transcribed it. That helped digitise newspaper archives and books. Later the words became house numbers from Street View photographs, and then the familiar grids: “select all squares with traffic lights”. Google described reCAPTCHA as channelling this effort into “digitizing text, annotating images, and building machine learning datasets”. hCaptcha went further and made labelling the business model: customers could pay to have their own images labelled by the visitors to other sites.

There are caveats. A single answer is a weak label, so it is checked against known answers and combined with many others. The puzzle is also a shrinking part of the test: modern versions score your mouse movements and browsing behaviour, and often show no challenge at all. And “free” is arguable: the site owner gets a free security service, and visitors pay for it in seconds of their time.

The irony is that image recognisers trained on this kind of data now solve the puzzles. A 2024 ETH Zurich study solved reCAPTCHA v2 image challenges 100% of the time with an off-the-shelf object detector. That is why CAPTCHAs are moving away from puzzles altogether.

Where did the training data for LLMs come from, and did anyone agree to it?

Short answer. Mostly from the public web, plus books, code, papers and licensed collections. For most of it, nobody was asked individually. Whether that is lawful is being settled now, in the courts and in regulation.

The bulk is crawled web text, heavily filtered for quality. Common Crawl, a non-profit archive, has collected petabytes of it since 2008. On top of that come Wikipedia, public code, scientific papers, books, forums and transcripts. Providers increasingly license data, such as news archives and forum content, and generate synthetic data with other models.

The legal position is still moving. In the US, a June 2025 ruling in a case brought by authors against Anthropic found that training on lawfully bought books was fair use but that keeping pirated copies was not; the case then settled for about $1.5 billion. Many other cases, including suits by news publishers filed since 2023, are still working through the courts. In the EU, providers of general-purpose models must have a copyright policy, respect machine-readable opt-outs from text and data mining, and publish a summary of their training content.

For you, this means asking vendors about copyright indemnities, and not assuming that output is free of other people's rights.

So who labels the data today?

Short answer. Mostly paid people, many of them outsourced, and increasingly experts and other models.

Labelling firms and platforms employ hundreds of thousands of annotators worldwide to draw boxes, transcribe and rate. Conditions vary widely. A 2023 TIME investigation reported that Kenyan workers were paid less than $2 an hour to label violent and abusive text so that a chatbot could learn to filter it. At the other end, preference tuning (RLHF) and evaluation now pay doctors, lawyers, engineers and PhD students to write and judge answers in their field, because ordinary raters can no longer tell a good answer from a merely plausible one. A growing share of the data is synthetic, with one model generating or grading examples for another.

In your own projects the labeller is usually your own expert, and their time is the main cost. It is also where your advantage lies: nobody else has your experts' labels.

The pattern behind most of these answers

The model is a fixed function that turns text into probable text. It does not look things up, it does not learn while you use it, and it does not keep what you send. The systems around it do store, log and act, and most of the risks and most of the fixes live in those systems, which are yours to design.
