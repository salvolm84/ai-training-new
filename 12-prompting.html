Module 12 · Part III — Applying it

Prompting

A workshop rather than a lecture. This is the skill that the largest number of people in your company will actually use, it takes about an hour to teach, and the difference between doing it badly and doing it well is roughly the difference between a novelty and a tool.

The frame that makes this click

Module 4 showed that the model predicts the most plausible continuation of the text in front of it. So a prompt is not a command — it is a description of the situation in which the answer you want would be the plausible thing to say. Everything below follows from that. You are not persuading the model; you are narrowing the space of plausible continuations.

12.3 · Six techniques, ranked by how much they pay

1 · Give the context it cannot have

The model does not know your company, your constraints, your reader or your standards. Almost every disappointing answer is a missing-context problem rather than a model problem.

Do: paste the relevant document, state who the reader is, say what has already been decided and what is out of scope. The most common improvement of all is simply pasting more of the actual material.

2 · Specify the output exactly

“Summarise this” gets you a summary of unknown length in an unknown shape. Say the shape: how many items, which fields, what order, how long, what to leave out.

Do: “A markdown table with columns Part, Finding, Severity (1–5), Action. One row per finding. No preamble.” Being this prescriptive feels rude and works extremely well.

3 · Show two or three examples

Few-shot prompting. One worked example conveys more about tone, granularity and format than a paragraph of description, because it is the format.

Do: include a real input and the exact output you would have written. Best technique for consistency across many runs, and the one people use least.

4 · Let it work before it answers

“Work through the calculation step by step before giving the answer” measurably improves multi-step reasoning, because each intermediate step becomes context for the next one (module 4.3).

Note for 2026: reasoning models do this internally, so the explicit instruction adds little with them and still helps with ordinary models. Ask for the working when you need to check it, which for anything numerical is always.

5 · Permit “I don't know”

Left to itself, the model produces the most plausible answer, and a plausible answer to an unanswerable question is an invention (module 4.3). Make the honest answer an explicitly available option.

Do: “If the documents do not contain the answer, reply exactly: NOT IN SOURCES. Do not use outside knowledge.” Cheapest hallucination reduction available.

6 · Split the task

A prompt asking for six things at once does all six adequately. Three prompts each asking for one thing do each one well, and you can inspect the intermediate results.

Do: extract, then analyse, then write. Section 12.4 has an exercise. This is also the difference between a fragile demo and a reliable process.

One more, for anything that matters

Ask for the sources and check them. “Quote the exact sentence you are relying on, with its clause number” turns a fluent claim into something verifiable in ten seconds. If it cannot produce the quotation, you have learned something important.

12.6 · What does not help

A lot of prompt folklore circulates. Some of it is real, some is cargo cult. Honest verdicts, with the reasoning.

How to tell folklore from technique

A real technique gives the model information or structure it did not have. Folklore tries to change its attitude. Context, examples, format specifications and decomposition are information. Flattery, threats, urgency and incantations are attitude — and while some of them produced measurable effects on older models, none of them is a reliable engineering practice. If you want to know whether something works for your task, you now know how to find out: the evaluation set from module 8.

12.7 · Run the workshop

Five exercises, about fifty minutes. People work on their own laptops with whatever approved tool you have; the value is in comparing results out loud after each one.

How to run it so it lands

After each exercise, ask two people to read out their prompt — not their result. The room learns far more from hearing how differently two people framed the same request than from seeing a good output. And keep exercise 5 to the end: discovering that the model will confidently invent a clause number is the lesson people carry back to their desks.

  • This module is the easiest sell of the fifteen and the one people request. Use that: it is a good first session for a sceptical department, with module 1 and the RAG demo from module 4.8.
  • Do not let it become a tips-and-tricks session. Anchor every technique back to “narrowing the space of plausible continuations” from the top of this page, or in six months people will be repeating incantations they do not understand.
  • Have a strong opinion ready on the myths section. People have read a great deal of nonsense and will be relieved to be told which parts they can stop doing.
  • Collect the prompts people write. They are the beginning of your organisation's prompt library and, more usefully, of the evaluation set from module 8.
