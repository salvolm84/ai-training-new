Module 8 · Part II — Engineering it

Evaluation

This is the module that turns a demo into engineering. Not “does it look good” but: how good, measured how, against what, with what uncertainty, and at what cost when it is wrong.

The discipline in one line

An AI system without an evaluation set is not a system — it is an anecdote. Build the evaluation before you build the model, keep it sealed, and re-run it on every change. Everything else in this module is detail on how.

8.1 · The four ways people avoid answering

“It gets it right nearly every time”

Measured on what? Usually on the examples the developer was looking at while building it, which is the definition of training data. Ask for a number, a denominator, and a description of the cases.

“98% accuracy”

On an imbalanced problem, as module 7.3 showed, this can be worse than doing nothing. Accuracy is the right metric only when the classes are balanced and the two kinds of error cost the same — which is almost never.

“It scores well on the benchmark”

Public benchmarks measure a different task than yours, and by 2026 many are partly contaminated — the test questions have leaked into training data. Useful for rough ranking of models, nearly worthless as evidence about your job.

“The users love it”

Worth knowing, and not the same as correct. People reliably prefer confident, fluent, agreeable answers — which is exactly the failure mode of a language model. Measure satisfaction and accuracy, separately.

The replacement question

“On N cases that the system has never seen, drawn from the same distribution as real work, it produced X correct, Y wrong in this direction and Z wrong in that direction. A person doing this task achieves B. Here is the confidence interval.” Any project that can produce that sentence is in good shape, whatever the numbers are.

8.2 · The threshold is a business decision

A classifier does not output a decision — it outputs a score. Turning that score into accept/reject requires a threshold, and choosing it means pricing your two kinds of error against each other. That is not a data-science choice, and it should not be made silently by a default value of 0.5.

What to do with this in practice

Put those two cost sliders in front of the person who owns the process, not the person who owns the model. They will argue about the numbers — that argument is the requirement, and it takes an afternoon. Then the threshold follows from arithmetic and can be documented, reviewed and audited. A threshold nobody chose is the most common quiet failure in deployed machine learning.

8.3 · ROC and precision–recall: the same model, two stories

Rather than pick one threshold, sweep all of them and plot the trade-off. Two standard curves do this, and on a rare-event problem they give dramatically different impressions of the same model. Knowing which one to ask for is a genuinely useful piece of professional literacy.

Why ROC flatters rare-event models

The false-positive rate divides by the number of healthy parts, which is enormous. Flagging 500 healthy parts out of 99,500 is a false-positive rate of 0.5% — invisible on an ROC curve — while being 500 unnecessary inspections and a precision of 9%. On imbalanced problems ask for the precision–recall curve. The ROC AUC will look impressive regardless.

And the honest defence of AUC

ROC AUC has one real virtue: it is independent of prevalence and of the threshold, so it measures the model's ranking ability alone. That makes it a fair way to compare two models. It is a poor way to decide whether a system is fit to deploy — for that you need the operating point and the costs from 8.2.

8.4 · Are the probabilities real?

A model that says “87% confident” is making a claim that can be checked: of all the cases where it said 87%, roughly 87% should actually be positive. A model where that holds is calibrated. Most are not, and it matters enormously, because every downstream decision — thresholds, escalation rules, expected-cost calculations, “only show the user answers above 90%” — silently assumes calibration.

The practical consequences
  • Accuracy and calibration are independent. Drag the slider: accuracy barely moves while calibration collapses. A model can rank cases perfectly and still lie about its confidence.
  • Deep networks are systematically overconfident, and the fix is cheap: temperature scaling on a held-out set — one parameter, fitted in seconds.
  • Language models are worse. The fluency of an answer carries no information about its correctness, and stated confidence (“I am fairly sure that…”) is generated text, not a measurement. Never treat it as one.

8.5 · What you have to beat

A number on its own means nothing. 92% is excellent, mediocre or embarrassing depending entirely on what the alternatives achieve — and the alternatives are often shockingly strong.

The four baselines to demand, every time
  1. Chance — random guessing at the observed class rate.
  2. Majority class — always predict the most common answer. On rare events this is brutally high (module 7.3).
  3. The simplest rule a human would write — one threshold on the most obvious feature. It is astonishing how often this gets within a few points of a neural network, and it costs a day.
  4. A person doing the task — measured, on the same cases. This is both the target and the ceiling implied by label noise (module 7.2).

8.6 · How many test cases do you actually need?

This is the question that separates people who measure from people who feel. You tested 50 cases and got 88%. Is that better than the 82% the old version scored? Very often the honest answer is you cannot tell.

Rules of thumb

Around 30–50 cases: enough to find obvious breakage. 200: enough to detect a 10-point difference. 1,000+: enough to argue about 2 points. Choose the size from the difference you need to detect, not from what is convenient.

The cheap trick that helps

Compare versions on the same cases and look at the cases where they differ, not at the two aggregate scores. A paired comparison needs far fewer examples, and the disagreements are also the most informative thing you will read all week.

And the trap

If you tweak the prompt, re-run the evaluation, keep the best, and repeat twenty times, you have tuned on your test set. It is now a validation set and it flatters you. Keep a second, smaller set sealed for the final number.

8.7 · Evaluating an LLM system

There is no accuracy score for “write a good answer”. But the problem is far more tractable than it first appears, because you are not evaluating a language model in general — you are evaluating your system on your task, and for that a list of real cases with written pass criteria does most of the work.

The lesson buried in the numbers

Version 2 is better on aggregate and worse on specific cases. That is the normal situation, and an aggregate score hides it completely. If one of the regressions happens to be a safety-relevant question, the “improved” version is not deployable however good the average looks. Always read the per-case diff, not just the totals — this is regression testing, and it is the single highest-value engineering practice for LLM systems.

How to build the evaluation set

Where cases come from

  • Real questions from real users — collect them from day one of any pilot.
  • The awkward cases your experts argue about.
  • Questions whose answer is not in your documents — the model must say so.
  • Every failure anyone reports. A bug becomes a permanent test case.
  • Adversarial cases: false premises, injected instructions, out-of-scope requests.

What a good pass criterion looks like

Not “is the answer good” but something a second person would grade the same way:

  • “States 92 Nm and cites ENG-STD-114 §4.2.”
  • “Declines to answer and says the documents do not cover it.”
  • “Mentions both the 2.0 mm limit and the stress-critical-zone exception.”
  • “Contains no part number that is not in the retrieved passages.”

Who grades

Humans, for the first version — and you need the inter-grader agreement from module 7.2 here too. Once criteria are sharp, much of it can be automated: string and citation checks for the objective parts, and a second model as a grader for the rest. Validate the automatic grader against human grades before trusting it, exactly as you would any other model.

What to run it on

Every prompt change, every model version change, every document re-index, before every release. It should take minutes and run in CI. A team that cannot re-run its evaluation in an afternoon will stop doing it within a month.
