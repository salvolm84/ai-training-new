Module 11 · Part III — Applying it

AI in engineering and simulation

Every previous module was general. This one is about the work your organisation actually does — and about the one application of machine learning that engineers tend to find genuinely exciting rather than merely interesting.

The pattern, in one sentence

You have a function that is expensive to evaluate — a CFD run, a structural solve, a test on a rig — and you need to evaluate it thousands of times to explore or optimise a design. Machine learning lets you learn a cheap approximation from a few hundred expensive evaluations, then explore in the approximation. That is the whole idea, it predates deep learning by decades, and it is where most of the engineering value is.

11.1 · Surrogate models

Suppose a single simulation of a cooling duct takes 45 minutes and returns one number — say efficiency — for one pair of design parameters. A full sweep of a 40×40 grid is 1,600 runs, or 50 days of cluster time. A surrogate changes the arithmetic: sample the design space sparsely, fit a model, and then evaluate that model a million times for free.

The surrogate here is radial-basis-function interpolation — closely related to kriging, which is what most commercial optimisation tools use. It fits by solving one linear system, needs no GPU and no training loop. A neural network would work too and is usually unnecessary at these sample counts; that is worth saying out loud.

Try 6 samples

The surrogate is confidently smooth and wrong in the interesting region. With very few samples you have not built a model of the physics — you have built a model of your ignorance, and it looks just as authoritative.

Try 40 samples

Mean error drops to around 5% of the output range — and the error map shows where it remains: at the sharpest peak and the domain edges. That is the honest, typical outcome. A few dozen runs buys you the shape of the whole design space; it does not buy you a trustworthy value at the most interesting point, which is why section 11.3 spends its runs there.

Then move the kernel width

Too narrow and the surrogate spikes at each sample and says nothing in between; too wide and it flattens out real features. This is the bias–variance trade-off from module 3.4 wearing different clothes.

11.2 · Where to put the samples

With a fixed budget of expensive runs, which runs you choose matters as much as the model you fit. This is design of experiments, and it is the cheapest thing on this page to get right.

Lab · Sampling plans compared

Computed live over 20 repeats

Median surrogate error against number of runs, for each plan, averaged over repeated draws.

Grid

Even coverage, but the number of runs explodes with dimensions: 5 levels in 6 variables is 15,625 runs. It also wastes samples by repeating the same value of each variable many times — with 25 grid points you only ever test 5 distinct values per parameter.

Random

Scales to any number of dimensions and needs no planning. Its weakness is clumping: with few samples you get holes in the space by pure luck, and you will not know where.

Latin hypercube

Every variable is sampled once in each of n equal strata, so the projection onto each parameter is perfectly uniform however few runs you use. Same cost as random, better coverage, one line of code. Use this by default.

11.3 · Optimising with a surrogate

The payoff is not a pretty contour plot — it is finding the best design with the fewest expensive runs. The strategy that works is iterative: fit a surrogate, ask it where the optimum is, spend one real solver run there, add the result, refit. Each run makes the surrogate sharper exactly where you care.

Exploration weight adds a bonus for sampling far from existing points. At zero the method exploits greedily and can converge on a local optimum; too high and it wanders. Real implementations do this more elegantly with a kriging model's own uncertainty estimate — the principle is identical.

The number that matters to a project manager

A 40×40 brute-force sweep is 1,600 solver runs. Surrogate-guided optimisation typically finds the same optimum in 20 to 40. At 45 minutes a run that is 50 days versus one day — and it is the difference between “we explored three candidate designs” and “we explored the space”.

11.4 · Physics-informed models

A pure data-driven fit knows nothing about physics, so with sparse noisy measurements it will happily produce a curve that violates conservation. The fix is elegant: add the governing equation to the loss function. The model is then penalised both for disagreeing with the sensors and for being physically impossible.

Loss = ‖ u(xi) − measuredi ‖² + λ · ‖ u″(x) − f(x) ‖² + boundary conditions fit the data · obey the equation · respect the boundaries

Below: a real example you can drive. A beam-like deflection problem with a known governing equation, five noisy sensors, and a nine-parameter model fitted by least squares. With the physics term switched off the problem is underdetermined: the fit chases the noise at the sensors and drifts between them. Switch it on and the same five measurements recover the true solution to within a fraction of a percent.

Why engineers should care

Physics constraints act as an enormous amount of free training data. Five sensors plus a differential equation beat fifty sensors without one. In practice this is what makes learned models viable in domains where measurements are expensive — and it means the result respects conservation instead of merely interpolating, which is the difference between a model an engineer will use and one they will not.

And the honest caveats
  • λ is another hyperparameter, and a badly chosen one makes training unstable.
  • For problems a classical solver handles well, a PINN is usually slower and less accurate. Their value is in inverse problems, sparse data, and awkward or unknown boundary conditions.
  • Getting the equation wrong now means being confidently, physically wrong.

Section 11.8 goes further: a real PINN you can train, where they fail and how people fix it, and the alternative of building the physics into the model's structure.

11.5 · The envelope problem

This is the single most important warning in the module, and the failure mode that has embarrassed the most engineering teams. A surrogate is an interpolator. Inside the region you sampled it is excellent. Outside it, it does not become uncertain — it becomes confidently wrong, and it does not tell you which regime you are in.

What to insist on, every time a surrogate is used
  1. The validity envelope is documented — the range of every input variable that was sampled.
  2. The model refuses, or flags loudly, outside it. A distance-to-nearest-sample check is five lines of code and prevents most of these incidents.
  3. Any design the surrogate proposes is verified with a real solver run before it influences a decision. One run. It is the cheapest insurance available.
  4. Nothing safety-relevant is signed off on a surrogate result alone. Use it to explore and to rank; use the solver and the test to decide.

11.6 · Synthetic data and the sim-to-real gap

Labelled real data is slow and expensive (module 7.7 puts a number on it). Simulated data is neither: a solver or a renderer produces as many labelled examples as you have cluster time for, with perfect labels and no privacy questions. So teams increasingly train on it:

Rendered images

CAD geometry rendered under varied lighting and pose, with defects painted in, to train an inspection model before the line has made enough faulty parts to learn from. Every pixel is labelled for free.

CFD and FE results

Every surrogate on this page is trained on simulated data. That is fine when the question is “reproduce the solver”, and less fine the moment the model is used to predict what the car will do.

Augmented telemetry

Real logs time-warped, noise-injected, or with simulated faults spliced in, so an anomaly detector sees failure modes the fleet has not produced yet.

The catch is in one sentence: a model trained on a simulator learns the simulator, including everything the simulator gets wrong. The difference between the simulated world and the real one is the domain gap. It comes from missing physics (tyre thermal behaviour, compliance, wear, a flow feature the mesh does not resolve), from idealised inputs (renders that are too clean, sensors without drift), and from operating conditions the simulation campaign never covered (section 11.5 again). None of it shows up in a test on more simulated data.

Calibrate with a little real data

The simulator gets the complicated structure right; what it misses is often smooth. So a handful of real measurements used to correct the simulator-trained model beats the same handful used on its own, by a wide margin. In a neural network the same idea is fine-tuning: pre-train on simulation, then retrain the last layers on real data. Watch the lower chart: the residual curve falls fast, real-only needs many more samples to catch up.

Domain randomisation

If you do not know the missing parameter, randomise it: train across a wide spread of friction, lighting, sensor gain or heat transfer, so the real world looks like one more variation. It buys robustness, not accuracy. Note what happens to the sim test error when you switch it on — it gets worse, because the model is no longer tuned to the nominal simulator. That is the point, and a reason not to judge the model on simulated data.

Always test on real data

Error on held-out simulation measures how well you learned the simulator. It says nothing about the gap. Keep a real test set that is never used for calibration, report sim and real error side by side, and treat the ratio as a number to track. If you have no real test data, you do not know how good the model is — only how good the simulator thinks it is.

  • Start with randomisation off, missing physics at 0.6 and zero real samples. Point at the two big numbers: the sim test says the model is excellent, the car says it is several times worse. That ratio is the domain gap, and it is invisible unless you measure on real data.
  • Then drag the real samples to about 5 with the residual model. Most of the gap closes. Switch to “real data only” at the same 5 samples — far worse. That comparison is the business case for keeping the simulator and buying a few real measurements, rather than choosing one or the other.
  • Set missing physics to zero to show the honest baseline: when the simulator is right, a model trained on it transfers almost perfectly, and the real samples add nothing but noise.
  • Domain randomisation helps most when you do not know how wrong the simulator is. Try missing physics at 0 and at 1 with it on: neither is perfect, neither is a disaster.

11.7 · Learning the physics: ML inside CFD and FEM

Every surrogate so far returned one number per design: an efficiency, a peak, a drag value. That is not how you look at a simulation. You look at the pressure on the underbody, the temperature map on a battery module, the stress around a fillet. This section is about machine learning applied to the field — the solution itself — and about the quieter option of putting the learning inside the solver instead of in place of it.

From a number to a field

The output is enormous

A scalar surrogate predicts one value. A field model predicts one value per mesh node — 10⁵ for a modest surface, 10⁷ or more for a volume. You cannot fit an independent model per node from a few hundred runs; the model has to learn the structure that links neighbouring nodes.

The mesh changes between designs

Change the geometry and the mesher gives you a different number of nodes, connected differently. Stacking results into a tidy table — what every method in modules 3 and 6 assumes — no longer works. Either you interpolate everything onto a common grid, or you use a model that works on the mesh directly.

The important part is small

A separation line, a hot spot, the peak stress at a notch: the features that decide a design occupy a tiny fraction of the domain. A model can be excellent on average and wrong exactly there. The third lab in this section is about that trap.

Reduced-order models: proper orthogonal decomposition

The classic method, in use for decades in fluids and structures, and the one to try first. Run the solver for a set of parameter values and store each solution field as a column — the snapshot matrix. A singular value decomposition of that matrix finds the handful of spatial patterns, the modes, that between them reproduce almost every snapshot. A new solution is then approximated as the mean field plus a weighted sum of those modes; only the weights depend on the parameters, so you interpolate a few numbers instead of a whole field.

T(x; μ) ≈ T̄(x) + Σk=1…r ak(μ) · φk(x) mean field · coefficients interpolated across the design parameters μ · modes from the SVD of the snapshots

Look at the energy curve

Two or three modes hold almost all of the variation in sixteen snapshots. That is why POD works: physical solutions over a family of designs are far less varied than their size suggests. Each mode is a field you can plot and argue about — the method is explainable by construction, which matters in a review.

Where it struggles

Things that move are expensive for POD: a travelling heat source, a shock, a separation point that shifts with the design. A shifted peak is a new pattern, so you need more modes and more snapshots. Raise β to its top training value and watch the error climb as the hot spot sharpens.

Outside the envelope

Press the button. The modes cannot represent a field they have never seen, and the coefficient interpolation is now an extrapolation — section 11.5 for a whole field at once. Note that the heat-balance error moves too: a physical check you can compute without knowing the true answer.

Graph networks and neural operators

POD needs every snapshot on the same mesh and handles a few parameters well. The two families of deep-learning field models aim at what it cannot do: geometry that changes and meshes that differ.

Mesh-based graph neural networks

Treat the mesh as a graph: nodes carry the local state, edges carry relative position. The network learns a local update rule — how a node is influenced by its neighbours — and applies it many times, which is structurally close to what a solver does. Because the rule is local, it runs on any mesh, including one it has never seen.

Promise: native on unstructured meshes, generalisation across related geometries. Cost: memory grows with the mesh, and long-range effects (pressure in incompressible flow) need many message-passing steps or a multi-scale variant.

Neural operators (FNO, DeepONet)

Learn a map from an input function (a geometry, a boundary condition, a load field) to an output function. The Fourier neural operator mixes information in frequency space on a regular grid; DeepONet uses one network to encode the input and another to evaluate the output at any query point.

Promise: train at one resolution, evaluate at another. Read that carefully: it means the model can be queried on a finer grid, not that it becomes more accurate than the data it was trained on.

Field models are typically accurate to a few per cent on designs like the training set — useful for screening and ranking, not for a value you sign off. The open question, as of 2026, is generalisation to genuinely new geometry: a model trained on one vehicle family says little about a different architecture, and nothing flags when it has stopped knowing.

ML inside the solver, rather than replacing it

The other route keeps the validated solver and uses learning to make it faster or to supply a piece of physics it lacks. Two very different levels of risk sit under this heading.

⇥

Warm starts and initial guesses

Start the iterative solver from a predicted field instead of a uniform one. The converged answer is whatever the solver converges to, so the ML can only cost or save iterations. The lab below makes this concrete.

▦

Learned preconditioners

A preconditioner reshapes the linear system so the solver converges in fewer steps; a learned one is tuned to the systems your workflow actually produces. Same safety property as a warm start — the solver still enforces the tolerance — and the same honest limit: gains are modest unless the classical preconditioner was poorly matched.

◫

Mesh adaptation

Predict where the mesh needs refining — wakes, boundary layers, notches — from the geometry or a coarse solution, instead of several adapt-and-resolve cycles. A bad prediction gives a worse mesh, which a mesh-convergence check still catches.

≋

Turbulence-model corrections for RANS

Learn a correction to the eddy viscosity or the Reynolds stresses from high-fidelity LES or DNS data. Here the ML changes the answer, so the risk is of a different kind. A closure trained on a family of separated flows has learned those flows; on a new geometry it can be worse than the baseline model, and nothing in the solution says so. Corrections also have to respect invariance and realisability, or they destabilise the solver.

⊞

Data-driven material models in FEM

Replace a fitted constitutive law with one learned from coupon tests or from micro-scale simulations of a composite or a lattice. The finite-element solver is unchanged; only the stress–strain routine is learned. The risks are strain states never tested (multiaxial, cyclic) and models that are not thermodynamically consistent — section 11.8 shows how to build that in.

∂

Physics-informed networks

The section 11.4 idea at full scale. Useful for inverse problems and sparse measurements, generally not faster than a classical solver for a forward problem. Section 11.8 goes into how they work, where they fail, and what has replaced them in some uses.

Why this is the pattern that gets past certification

Look at the last number: the two final answers agree to the solver tolerance whatever initial guess you pick. The ML model cannot change the result, only how long it takes to get there — and a bad model costs a few iterations, not a wrong design. That is a claim you can make to a certification engineer without a new validation campaign, which is why warm starts, preconditioners and mesh prediction are where field ML enters production first. Note also what the lab shows honestly: the gain is a factor of two or three for tight tolerances, more for loose ones, and with conjugate gradients it can be close to nothing — CG is already good at the smooth error a ROM removes.

The trust question for fields

A field model's headline accuracy is almost always an average over the domain. Engineers rarely care about the average. The last lab is the problem in its purest form: the stress field around a hole in a plate under tension, where the peak at the edge of the hole is three times the nominal stress — the textbook stress concentration — and a smooth model of the whole field.

What to check before believing a field model
  1. The quantities you design to, separately from the average. Peak stress and its location, peak temperature, separation and reattachment points, integrated forces. Report them as their own error lines; an RMS over the domain is not evidence about any of them.
  2. The sign of the error at the critical point. Smooth models shave peaks, so they tend to under-predict exactly where under-prediction is unsafe. Non-conservative by default.
  3. Conservation. Mass, momentum and energy balances, net forces against reactions. These can be checked on every prediction without knowing the true answer, and a violated balance is a strong signal that the model is out of its depth.
  4. Where the training data is dense. Refining samples at the critical feature helps, as the lab shows — and moves error elsewhere. Your loss function decides where the error goes.
  5. A solver run on the final design, as section 11.5 insisted for scalar surrogates. For a field model it is the only check that covers what you did not think to check.

  • Expected: “What about the commercial tools that claim this?” — Ask three questions: error on held-out designs the vendor did not choose, error at the peak rather than averaged, and behaviour on a geometry outside the training family. Then run your own test with your own runs before buying.

11.8 · Physics-informed and physics-constrained learning: the research frontier

Optional deep dive · about 15 minutes · as of 2026

This section goes further than the rest of the module and can be skipped in a shorter delivery. It is for the people in the room who will read the papers, evaluate the vendor claims, or supervise a thesis student. Section 11.4 showed the idea in one lab; this one covers how it works, where it fails, the alternatives that build physics into the model's structure, and which parts are ready for engineering use.

Physics-informed neural networks in detail

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) is an ordinary network uθ(x, t) whose loss includes the governing equation. Training penalises three things at once:

L(θ) = wd · mean ‖uθ(xi) − datai‖² + λ · mean ‖𝒩[uθ](xc) − f(xc)‖² + wb · mean ‖boundary and initial conditions‖² misfit at the sensors · PDE residual at collocation points xc · boundary terms

The derivatives inside 𝒩 — ∂u/∂x, ∂²u/∂x², ∂u/∂t — are derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs, obtained by automatic differentiation; no mesh is involved. Collocation points are just places in the domain where the residual is evaluated, and they can be anywhere.

Forward problem

Equation, coefficients and boundary conditions known; find u. This is what your solver already does, and a PINN is usually the slower, less accurate way to do it.

Inverse problem

Some coefficient is unknown — a conductivity, a damping value, a heat-transfer coefficient — and you have sparse sensors. Make the coefficient a trainable parameter alongside the weights. This is where PINNs earn their keep.

Data assimilation

Measurements and an imperfect model combined into a best estimate of the full state: reconstruct a temperature field from a dozen thermocouples, or a flow field from a few pressure taps. Classical alternatives (Kalman filters, adjoint methods) exist and should be the baseline.

The honest verdict on PINNs

For a well-posed forward problem that a finite-volume or finite-element code already solves, a PINN is generally slower to train than the solver is to run, and less accurate. Their value is in inverse problems, in sparse, noisy data with partly known physics, and in problems where meshing is the obstacle. If someone proposes a PINN for a forward problem, ask what the solver baseline was and how long it took.

Hard constraints versus soft penalties

A loss-function penalty asks the model to respect the physics and lets it trade that against fitting the data. The alternative is to build the physics into the architecture, so the model cannot violate it whatever its weights are.

Divergence-free by construction

Predict a stream function ψ (2-D) or a vector potential (3-D) and take derivatives: u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x is incompressible for any ψ. The lab below does exactly this.

Conservative fluxes

Predict fluxes across cell faces, as a finite-volume scheme does, and update each cell by the net flux. Whatever leaves one cell enters its neighbour, so the total is conserved exactly.

Symmetry and equivariance

If rotating the input should rotate the output, use a network that does that by construction — E(3)-equivariant graph networks in molecular and particle simulation are the established example. It removes a whole class of errors and usually reduces the data needed.

Energy-conserving dynamics

Hamiltonian and Lagrangian neural networks learn a scalar energy or Lagrangian and derive the motion from it, so a learned undamped system conserves energy instead of drifting. Damping and forcing are then added explicitly.

Thermodynamically consistent materials

For FEM material models: learn a strain-energy function with an input-convex network, or a polyconvex form for hyperelasticity, and derive stress from it. The model is then stable, objective and dissipation-free by construction — properties a free-form network of stress against strain does not have.

The trade-off

Hard constraints restrict what the model can represent. That is the point when the constraint is right; when it is wrong (the flow is not incompressible, the material is dissipative) the model cannot fit the truth at all. Choose constraints you would defend in a design review.

Hybrids: keep the physics model, learn the gap

Physics-informed neural operators

A neural operator (section 11.7) trained on solver data and a PDE residual. The residual term reduces the data needed and lets the model be refined on a new case without new solver runs. Research-stage, with promising results on benchmark flows.

Differentiable solvers and solver-in-the-loop

Write the solver so gradients flow through it, then train a network to correct a coarse solver step by step, with the loss measured after many steps through the solver. The network learns what the coarse mesh misses, and the solver keeps the result stable and physical. Promising for accelerating unsteady simulation; needs a differentiable code, which most commercial ones are not.

Universal differential equations and neural ODEs

Keep the equations you trust and add a small learned term for the part you do not: dx/dt = fknown(x) + NN(x). The network is trained through an ODE solver. The learned term is small and inspectable, and it can sometimes be distilled into a formula — see equation discovery below.

Grey-box models for vehicles

The engineering version of the same idea, and the most mature: a physical vehicle-dynamics or thermal network model with a learned correction for tyre behaviour, an unmodelled heat path or a component nonlinearity. It is section 11.6's residual model applied inside a dynamic model, and it inherits that section's advice: test on real data.

Discovering the equations

Sometimes the aim is not a model that predicts but the equation itself. Symbolic regression searches over formulas for one that fits the data. Sparse identification (SINDy) is the tractable version: build a library of candidate terms — 1, x, v, x², xv, x³ … — and find the fewest terms that reproduce the measured derivatives, by least squares with small coefficients repeatedly set to zero. The result is a readable equation an engineer can check against physical intuition.

When equation discovery is realistic

A few state variables, clean or well-filtered measurements, derivatives you can estimate, and a library that contains the right terms. Low-dimensional mechanical, thermal and electrical systems on a rig fit that description well, and the result is an equation you can put into an existing simulation tool.

When it is not

Turbulent flows, many interacting variables, noisy derivatives, or a library that is missing the true term — tick the box in the lab and it confidently finds a different equation that fits nearly as well. It cannot discover a term you did not think to offer it, and a sparse answer is not automatically a true one.

The frontier and its open problems, as of 2026

Foundation models for PDEs

Models pre-trained on solutions of many equations, then fine-tuned on a new one with few examples — the module 18 pattern applied to physics. Published results (for example Poseidon, 2024) show real sample-efficiency gains on fluid benchmarks. Benchmarks are mostly regular grids and simple geometry; industrial meshes and certification-grade accuracy are not yet what they are measured on.

Weather: the proof that learned simulators can win

Learned weather models now match or beat physics-based forecasts on many headline scores, and ECMWF has run its machine-learned AIFS operationally since February 2025, alongside its physics model. Why that does not transfer automatically to vehicle CFD: weather has decades of consistent global reanalysis data on one fixed geometry, the planet. Engineering has thousands of runs, on geometry that changes every project, and a certification process that asks why.

Uncertainty-aware physics ML

Bayesian PINNs, ensembles of operators and conformal intervals on field outputs. A field model that reports where it does not know is far more useful than one that does not; module 16 covers the methods, and the gap between research and tooling is still wide.

Generalisation across geometry

The central open problem for engineering use. Models interpolate well within a family of designs and degrade on a new one, without warning. Expect progress claims here to be the ones that matter most and need the most scrutiny.

Benchmarking

A 2024 systematic review (McGreivy & Hakim) found that most papers claiming ML beats a standard numerical method for fluid-related PDEs had compared against a weak baseline — an unoptimised solver, or one run at a higher accuracy than needed — and found evidence of reporting bias. Treat speed-up claims as unproven until the baseline is one your own CFD team would accept.

How to read a claim

Use the checklist in module 14.5, and add four questions specific to physics ML: what was the solver baseline, at what accuracy and on what hardware; was training time counted; is the test geometry outside the training family; and is the error reported at the quantity you design to, or averaged over the domain.

11.9 · The other patterns worth knowing

◲

Vision inspection

A convolutional network on inspection images (module 3.5). The mature, boring, high-return application.

The real work: consistent labelling (module 7.2), lighting and fixture repeatability, and the cost trade-off in module 8.2. Where defects are rare or unlabelled, invert it into anomaly detection on good parts (module 6.6).

∿

Telemetry anomaly detection

Learn what a healthy run looks like from your enormous archive of uneventful data, then flag deviation — on a rig, a vehicle, a machine tool or a whole line.

Why it works here: no labelled failures required, and you already own the data. The hard part is defining “healthy” across operating conditions, and the alarm-rate trade-off from module 6.6.

◈

Generative and topology-driven design

State loads, constraints and objectives; let an algorithm propose geometries — classically by topology optimisation, increasingly with generative models proposing candidates that a solver then evaluates.

Realistic framing: it widens the set of candidates a human considers. It does not replace the engineer, and manufacturability constraints are where most of these proposals die.

◱

Digital twins

A live model of a specific physical asset, updated with its own sensor data — for state estimation, remaining-life prediction and what-if analysis.

Honest note: the term covers everything from a dashboard to a full physics model with data assimilation. Ask which one is meant before agreeing to build one, and ask what decision it will change.

⌸

Meshing, solvers and pre-processing

Learned models that accelerate the unglamorous parts: mesh generation, initial guesses for iterative solvers, turbulence closure, super-resolution of coarse simulation fields.

Why this is the quiet winner: a 3× speed-up of an existing validated workflow needs no new sign-off process, and compounds across every project that uses it. Section 11.7 has a lab on exactly this.

▤

Engineering knowledge retrieval

The RAG pattern from module 4.8 applied to standards, test reports, deviation records, lessons learned and supplier documentation.

Why it is usually the best first project: minimal risk under module 10, no labelling, immediate value, and it surfaces your document-governance problems early — which you need to fix anyway.

If you are choosing one place to start

Start with a surrogate for a simulation your team already runs repeatedly, or retrieval over your own engineering documents. Both use data you already have, need no labelling campaign, sit outside the heavy regulatory tiers, and produce a number a project manager can understand: solver-days saved, or hours of searching saved. Vision inspection is higher value and a much bigger project — do it second, with a labelling budget.

11.10 · Validation, and the conversation with certification

Simulation earned its place in engineering through decades of verification and validation practice. Learned models have to earn it the same way, and the good news is that the framework already exists — you are adding a component to it, not inventing a new discipline.

How a surrogate fits V&V

  • Verification: does the surrogate reproduce the solver it was trained on, within a stated tolerance, on held-out runs it never saw? This is module 8, and it is straightforward because you can generate as much test data as you can afford.
  • Validation: does the underlying solver reproduce reality? Unchanged by the surrogate — and note that the surrogate can never be more valid than the solver it imitates.
  • Uncertainty: report surrogate error as an additional, separate contribution to the total uncertainty budget. Do not fold it into solver error and hope.
  • Envelope: stated explicitly, and enforced in the tooling (section 11.5).
  • Synthetic training data: if any of it came from simulation or augmentation, report error on real data separately from error on simulated data (section 11.6).
  • Field outputs: report error at the quantities you design to — peak values and their location, integrated forces, conservation balances — not only an average over the domain (section 11.7).

What makes certification people comfortable

  • The learned model informs a decision; a validated method makes it. Use the surrogate to explore and rank, the solver and the test to confirm.
  • Determinism: a fixed model version, fixed seeds, versioned training data, reproducible outputs. Non-determinism is the thing they object to most, and it is largely avoidable.
  • Traceability: which model version, trained on which runs, produced which number, on which date. Exactly the module 8 rerun procedure.
  • A documented failure mode and detection: what happens when it is wrong, and how you find out.

Have this conversation at the start of the project, not at sign-off. The answer is rarely no — but it is usually “not in that role, and not without that documentation”.
