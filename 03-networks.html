3.1 · Build one and train it

A layer is a row of neurons that all look at the same inputs. A network is layers in sequence: the outputs of one layer become the inputs of the next. The layers in the middle are called hidden, for the unhelpful reason that you do not observe them directly.

And this

  • Run XOR with 1 hidden neuron first (it cannot), then 2 (it can). That single click is the payoff for module 2's frustration. Make a moment of it.
  • Watch the loss curve, not the accuracy: it drops in plateaus and sudden cliffs. Real training runs look exactly like this, which is why people stare at these curves for a living.
  • With ReLU on Spiral you will occasionally see a boundary made of straight facets — a visible reminder that ReLU networks are piecewise linear. The curve is an illusion built from flat pieces.

3.3 · Backpropagation: assigning blame

Training needs an answer to one question: if I nudge this particular weight, by how much does the total error change? For a network with a hundred million weights, answering that question a hundred million separate times is impossible. Backpropagation answers it for every weight in a single backward sweep, at roughly twice the cost of the forward pass.

Lab · The backward sweep

Walk the five stages
Why it is called the chain rule

If the error depends on the output, the output depends on the last layer, and the last layer depends on the one before it, then the sensitivity of the error to an early weight is the product of the sensitivities along the path. Multiply the local slopes together and you have it. Two consequences follow directly: multiply many numbers smaller than 1 and the signal vanishes (vanishing gradients — the reason for ReLU); multiply many numbers larger than 1 and it explodes (hence gradient clipping). Most of the engineering in deep learning is management of that product.

  • Analogy that lands with engineers: a factory line produces a defective part. You do not test every machine independently — you trace the fault backwards through the line, and each station tells you how much of the deviation it contributed. Backpropagation is that trace, done with derivatives.
  • Emphasise the cost asymmetry: one forward pass, one backward pass, and you have the gradient for every parameter. Without that efficiency, nothing since 1986 would exist.

Underfitting

Degree 1. The model is too rigid to represent the pattern, so it is wrong on training and test data. Both errors are high and similar. The fix is a bigger model or better features.

The sweet spot

Around degree 3–4 here. Test error is at its minimum. You find it not by theory but by measuring on held-out data — which is why the train/validation/test split is non-negotiable.

Overfitting

Degree 12–14. The curve passes almost exactly through every training point, so train error is near zero — and it thrashes wildly between them. Train error alone would tell you this is your best model. It is your worst.

The single most common way an AI project fails

Somebody reports 98% accuracy. It was measured on data the model trained on, or on data that leaked across the split — the same vehicle appearing in both sets, the same document chunked into both. Always ask: accuracy on what data, and had the model ever seen it? Nothing else in the report matters until that is answered.

Go deeper · but modern models have billions of parameters and twelve data points' worth of intuition does not apply

Correct, and it is a genuinely interesting wrinkle. The classical U-curve above predicts that a model with far more parameters than data points should be useless. Very large neural networks are not useless — a phenomenon sometimes called “double descent”: as capacity grows past the point of interpolating the training data exactly, test error can start falling again.

Why, roughly: among the many parameter settings that fit the training data perfectly, gradient descent does not pick one at random. It has a bias towards simple, smooth solutions, and techniques like weight decay, dropout and data augmentation strengthen that bias. So enormous models behave as though they were smaller and better-regularised than their parameter count suggests.

What has not changed: you still cannot evaluate on training data, overfitting is still real when data is scarce, and for LLMs the equivalent failure is memorisation — reproducing training text verbatim, which is both a quality problem and a legal one.

3.5 · Matching the architecture to the data

A plain stack of fully-connected layers works on inputs with no structure. Most real data has structure: an image has neighbourhoods, a signal has time order, a graph has connectivity. Every famous architecture is one idea about which structure to build into the wiring so the network does not have to learn it from scratch.

The convolution, in your hands

Images are the clearest case. A fully-connected layer on a 1-megapixel image would need a million weights per neuron, and it would have to learn separately that an edge in the top-left corner is the same thing as an edge in the middle. A convolution fixes both problems at once: use one small filter, and slide it everywhere.

Key takeaway

In a real CNN nobody types those nine numbers — they are weights, learned by gradient descent like any other. What the engineer chooses is the structure: filter size, how many filters, how deep. Train one on photographs and the first layer reliably learns edge detectors that look almost exactly like the ones you just selected by hand. Later layers combine them into corners, then textures, then objects. That hierarchy is the whole reason deep learning beat hand-engineered vision.

Now break it deliberately

Train it on circles drawn in the centre only, then draw a circle in the corner. It fails. The network never learned “circle” — it learned “dark pixels in the middle”, because that is what your data said. This is not a toy artefact: it is the single most common reason production models fail. The model learns your dataset, not your intention.

And notice the effort

Drawing eight examples took a minute and was tedious. A production vision model needs tens of thousands, each labelled consistently by someone who knows the acceptance standard. When you budget a machine-learning project, the labelling is the project — usually more expensive than the modelling by a wide margin.

  • Let one person from the audience draw. Their handwriting is different from yours, which is the whole point about data distribution — demonstrate it live by testing their drawings against your trained model.
  • Say out loud that this is a real network with real backpropagation, 4,000-odd parameters, trained in under a second. The mechanism is identical to a frontier model; only the scale differs by eight orders of magnitude.
