Module 6 · Part II — Engineering it

Beyond neural networks

The previous modules were about deep learning, because that is what makes the news. Most of the value available to a manufacturing company is in the methods in this module — which are older, cheaper, easier to explain, and frequently more accurate.

The claim this module defends

On tabular data — rows and columns, the shape of every database export and every sensor log you have — a tree ensemble usually beats a neural network, trains in seconds instead of hours, needs no GPU, and tells you which columns mattered. You will see that head to head in section 6.4 with both models trained live. Reaching for deep learning by default is the most common technical mistake in corporate AI.

6.1 · Three genuinely different questions

Supervised

“What is the answer for this one?”

You have examples with known answers. The model learns the mapping. Two flavours: classification (which category) and regression (what number).

You need: labels. That is the whole cost.

Unsupervised

“What is in here?”

No answers, only data. The model finds structure: groups (clustering), a compressed description (dimensionality reduction), or things that do not fit (anomaly detection).

You need: data you already have. This is the underused half.

Reinforcement

“What should I do next?”

No answers and no fixed dataset — an environment, actions, and a reward that may arrive much later. The model learns a policy by trying things.

You need: a simulator, or a very tolerant real process. Section 6.7.

A useful test when someone describes a project: what exactly is the label? If nobody can answer in one sentence, it is not a supervised learning project yet — it is either an unsupervised problem, or a data-collection project wearing a machine-learning costume.

6.2 · Decision trees: twenty questions, learned

A decision tree asks a sequence of threshold questions about one feature at a time — is the coolant temperature above 96°C? — and each answer narrows down the region of input space you are in. Training means choosing which questions to ask, in which order.

The criterion is impurity. A group of samples that is all one class has impurity zero; a 50/50 mix is maximally impure. A good split is one where the two resulting groups are each purer than the parent. That reduction is called the information gain, and the algorithm simply tries every feature and every threshold and keeps the best.

The learned rules

Why engineers like trees

Read the box above: those are if-then rules you could put in a specification. A tree is a learned expert system. It handles mixed units without scaling, copes with missing values, is unbothered by monotone transformations, and tells you exactly why it rejected a part. No other high-accuracy method is this transparent.

And why one tree is not enough

Push the depth to 10 and watch test accuracy fall while train accuracy hits 100% — the overfitting from module 3, in a new costume. A single tree is also unstable: move a few points and the whole structure changes, because an early split commits everything below it. The fix is next.

6.3 · Ensembles: many weak models, one strong one

One deep tree overfits. The insight of the 1990s was that many shallow, deliberately imperfect trees, combined, do not. There are two ways to combine them, and both are still state of the art on tabular data thirty years later.

Random forest — vote in parallel

Train many trees, each on a random subsample of the rows and a random subset of the columns, then average their votes. Each tree is mediocre and wrong in its own direction; the errors partially cancel. Hard to overfit, trivially parallel, almost no tuning.

Use when you want a strong result in ten minutes with default settings.

Gradient boosting — correct in sequence

Train a small tree. Look at what it got wrong. Train the next tree on the remaining error. Repeat a few hundred times, adding each correction with a small weight. Each tree is tiny; the sum is extremely accurate.

Use when you want the best number on a tabular problem. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are implementations of this idea and they win most tabular competitions.

What to conclude, carefully

Not “neural networks are bad”. The honest conclusion is that architecture should follow the structure of the data — the same lesson as module 3.5. Tabular data has no spatial or sequential structure to exploit, so the tree's ability to carve axis-aligned regions is a better inductive bias than the network's ability to draw smooth curves. Reverse the situation — images, text, audio, time series — and the network wins by an enormous margin. The skill is knowing which situation you are in.

  • This section exists to save the company money. Run it live, twice, and let the room see the forest win on a dataset that looks like their data.
  • If someone objects that real tabular problems have 200 columns rather than 2: correct, and the gap widens in the trees' favour, because a neural network then needs careful scaling, encoding of categoricals and regularisation, while a boosted ensemble needs none of it.
  • Have the answer ready for “so when should we use deep learning on tabular data?” — when you have an enormous dataset, when you need to fuse tabular data with text or images in one model, or when you want embeddings of high-cardinality categories.

6.5 · Clustering: structure without labels

You have 40,000 warranty claims and no categories. Clustering proposes categories. The most used algorithm, k-means, is three lines: put k markers down at random, assign every point to its nearest marker, move each marker to the centre of its points, repeat.

Inertia against k — the “elbow” is the usual, unsatisfying way of choosing k.

Three honest warnings
  • k-means always succeeds. Give it structureless noise and it will still return clusters, confidently. The algorithm has no concept of “there are no groups here”.
  • You choose k. Re-seed the centroids a few times and watch the result change. The elbow plot is a heuristic, not an answer.
  • It assumes round, similar-sized blobs. Elongated or nested groups defeat it — use DBSCAN or a Gaussian mixture instead.

6.6 · Anomaly detection: the one that needs no labelled faults

This deserves to be much better known in industry. The usual blocker for defect detection is that you have very few examples of failure — sometimes none. Anomaly detection inverts the problem: learn what normal looks like, then flag anything that is not it. Your enormous archive of uneventful production data becomes the training set.

Tighten the threshold and you catch everything, including 200 healthy parts a day that someone now has to re-inspect. Loosen it and the line runs smoothly while defects escape. There is no setting that avoids the choice — and the correct setting depends on the relative cost of a false alarm and a missed fault, which is a business decision, not a data-science one. 6.7 · Reinforcement learning: learning to act

The third paradigm, and the one behind AlphaGo, most robotics research, and — since 2024 — the training of reasoning models. There is no dataset of correct answers. There is a world, a set of possible actions, and a reward that usually arrives long after the action that earned it.

The classic method, Q-learning, keeps one number for every (state, action) pair: the total future reward it expects if it takes that action here. Every time it acts, it nudges that number towards what actually happened plus what it now expects next. That single update rule, applied a few thousand times, produces a policy.

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α · [ r + γ · max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

No exploration at all, and it still solves the maze. The reason is subtle and worth knowing: every Q value starts at 0 while every step costs −0.02, so any action the agent has not yet tried looks better than one it has. Optimistic initialisation is doing the exploring for free.

Now switch to pessimistic (−1)

With ε = 0 it now fails essentially every time: the first route it stumbles into looks better than the untried alternatives, so it commits to it forever. Raise ε and it recovers — around 0.2 it succeeds about half the time, and by 0.5 reliably. That is exploration versus exploitation, and this pair of settings is the cleanest demonstration of it you will get.

And an honest note on γ

On a maze this small, γ barely matters — every value from 0.5 to 0.99 converges in 30 to 50 episodes, and you can verify that yourself. γ encodes how far ahead the agent cares, and it bites when the reward is far away or the episode is long. Do not take a parameter's importance on faith because a textbook stressed it; on your problem, measure it.

Why this is much harder in reality

This gridworld has 36 states and the agent may die, for free, thousands of times. A real process has effectively infinite states and each failure costs money, hardware or safety. That is why industrial reinforcement learning runs in a simulator — and why the quality of the simulator becomes the real project, which puts you back in module 11.

Go deeper · reward hacking, and why it matters beyond robotics

An RL agent optimises the reward you wrote, not the outcome you wanted. The literature is full of agents that found degenerate solutions: a boat-racing agent that learned to spin in circles collecting respawning bonus points instead of finishing the race; simulated robots that exploited physics-engine bugs to move without walking. The agent was not malfunctioning — the reward function was an incomplete description of the goal.

This is not a curiosity. The preference-tuning stage of every modern LLM (module 4.6) is reinforcement learning against a learned reward model, and the same failure appears as sycophancy: the model learned that agreeable answers score well, which is true and not what anyone wanted. Whenever you specify a metric for a learning system to maximise, assume it will find the cheapest way to maximise exactly that.

Key takeaway

Start at the top of that table and move down only when the simpler method has demonstrably failed on your data. A logistic regression that works is worth more than a transformer that might — it trains in seconds, you can explain it to an auditor, and it gives you the baseline that tells you whether the complicated thing was worth building.
