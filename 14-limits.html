Module 14 · Part III — Applying it

Limits and open questions

A course that ends on capability leaves people either credulous or cynical. This module is about what these systems genuinely cannot do, why, what nobody knows yet, and how to read the claims you will be sent for the next two years.

14.1 · What they cannot do, and why

Not a list of things they are bad at — those change every six months. A list of limitations that follow from how the systems work, which is why each one comes with a mechanism from an earlier module rather than an anecdote.

Know what they do not know

A model produces the most plausible continuation. Plausibility and truth are correlated in the training data and they are not the same thing, so an unanswerable question yields a confident, well-formed answer rather than an admission.

Mechanism: the training objective (module 4.3). Retrieval, explicit refusal permission and calibration (module 8.4) reduce it substantially. Nothing eliminates it, because there is no internal signal that distinguishes “I recall this” from “this is what such a sentence usually looks like”.

Reliably count, spell or calculate

Multi-step arithmetic, unit conversion, counting characters, tolerance stack-ups. A model may get these right and you cannot rely on it.

Mechanism: text arrives as sub-word tokens, so individual characters and digits are not separately visible (module 4.1), and there is no arithmetic unit — the answer is produced by pattern completion. The fix is architectural, not a better prompt: give it a calculator or code execution and require it to use them.

Distinguish your instructions from the data they read

Everything arrives as one stream of text. Delimiters and system prompts help and are not a security boundary.

Mechanism: module 9.4. This is the one genuinely unsolved security problem in the field, and the reason agent permissions matter more than agent quality.

Learn from your correction

Explaining a mistake fixes it for the rest of the conversation and not beyond. Tomorrow it will make the same error.

Mechanism: the weights are frozen at training time; the context window is working memory, not learning (module 4.7). Persistence has to be built by you — a prompt library, a retrieval store, or fine-tuning. The system does not improve by being used.

Explain why they produced a particular answer

A model asked to justify itself produces a plausible justification. That is not the same as a report of the computation that occurred, and research has found cases where the stated reasoning demonstrably does not match what drove the answer.

Mechanism: the explanation is generated text, subject to the same plausibility objective as everything else. This matters directly for anything you must justify to an auditor: a model's stated reasoning is evidence about its style, not about its processing.

Give you the same answer twice

Sampling, batching and hardware non-determinism mean identical input can produce different output, even at temperature zero.

Mechanism: module 4.3. Mitigate with low temperature, pinned versions and full logging of input and output. If your process needs bit-exact reproducibility, a language model is the wrong component for that step.

Sustain a long task without drifting

Performance on tasks requiring dozens of dependent steps remains far below what per-step accuracy suggests — the subject of section 14.3.

Mechanism: errors compound, and the system has no reliable way to notice that it has gone wrong several steps ago (module 9.3, the runaway loop). This has improved substantially and is the main practical limit on autonomous agents today.

Know anything that is not in the data

Your unreleased design, last Tuesday's decision, the thing your most experienced colleague has never written down.

Mechanism: obvious once stated, and constantly forgotten. It is why retrieval (module 4.8) matters more than model choice for corporate work, and why the organisations that get the most from this are the ones that had already written things down.

And one that is often claimed as a limit but is not

“It cannot be creative / it only recombines what it has seen.” This is too confident in both directions. The models demonstrably produce combinations no human wrote, which is useful; and they are optimised for plausibility, which pulls towards the conventional. The practically true statement is narrower: they are excellent at producing many candidate ideas and poor at judging which is good — which makes them a strong divergent tool and a weak convergent one, and tells you exactly where to put the human.

Why demos mislead

A five-step demo at 95% per step works 77% of the time — often enough to look impressive on stage. The same system on a thirty-step real task works 21% of the time. Neither number is a lie; they are the same system.

Which is why recovery matters more than accuracy

Move the recovery slider. Detecting and retrying failures buys more than improving the per-step rate, and it is far easier to engineer: validation, retries, checkpoints, and a human at the points where an error would be expensive.

And why short tasks are the business case

The reliable applications in 2026 are the ones with few dependent steps and a human at the end. That is not a limitation to work around — it is where the value currently is, and it is a great deal of value.

14.3 · We do not know how they work

Worth stating plainly, because it is easy to assume otherwise. We know exactly what operations a model performs — module 4 walked through every one. We do not know why a particular set of weights produces a particular answer, in the sense that you could explain it, predict it, or certify it.

What is actually known

  • Individual attention heads with identifiable jobs — tracking the previous token, matching brackets, resolving references (module 4.4).
  • Interpretable features can be extracted from the internal activations, and amplifying or suppressing one changes behaviour in a way that matches its apparent meaning. This line of work has advanced considerably.
  • Factual associations can be localised, to a degree, and edited.
  • Enough to build useful safety and monitoring tools — and not enough to explain a single answer end to end.

Why it matters to you specifically

  • Certification asks why a system will behave correctly, not just that it did on a test set. For learned components the honest answer today is statistical, not mechanistic — which is exactly why module 11.10 places the surrogate in an informing role rather than a deciding one.
  • Debugging is empirical. You cannot read the weights to find the fault; you build test cases and observe (module 8).
  • Guarantees are unavailable. You get measured error rates and bounded consequences instead, which is a legitimate engineering position — you just have to say it out loud rather than implying more.

14.4 · What the disagreement is actually about

Serious, well-informed people disagree sharply about where this goes, and the disagreement is narrower and more interesting than the public argument suggests. Here are the three positions as their own defenders would put them, rather than as their opponents characterise them. I am not going to tell you which is right, because I do not know and neither does anyone else.

The scaling case

It keeps going

  • Every predicted wall so far — data, context length, reasoning, multi-step tasks — has been pushed back further than sceptics expected, repeatedly.
  • Capability improvements have followed smooth, forecastable curves across many orders of magnitude. That is unusual and it suggests something general is being learned.
  • When pre-training gains slowed, a new axis appeared: spending compute at answer time. Expect further axes.
  • Most claimed limitations turn out to be about scaffolding — tools, memory, verification — rather than about the models, and scaffolding is ordinary engineering.

What would change their mind: two or three years of substantial compute increases producing little capability gain, across several labs.

The middle case

Transformative in a narrow band

  • The models become extremely good at tasks with abundant training data and checkable answers — code, translation, structured extraction, routine analysis — and stay unreliable elsewhere.
  • Most economic value comes not from better models but from integration: data, workflow, permissions, evaluation. That work is slow, organisational and unglamorous.
  • Verification cost becomes the binding constraint. If a human must check everything, savings cap out regardless of how good the model is.
  • Diffusion takes a decade because organisations, regulations and skills change slowly — as with electrification and computing.

What would change their mind: systems that reliably complete long, unsupervised, consequential work — or, in the other direction, a plateau in the narrow band too.

The sceptical case

Something is missing

  • Predicting text is not the same as understanding, and the systems fail in ways that suggest no stable world model: confident errors on simple variations of problems they otherwise solve.
  • Benchmark progress is partly contamination and partly optimisation against the benchmark. Independent, uncontaminated evaluation shows a smaller gap than headlines do.
  • High-quality training data is finite, and synthetic data risks compounding the model's own errors.
  • Reliability has not improved as fast as capability, and reliability is what production requires. Section 14.2 is the reason.

What would change their mind: robust performance on genuinely novel problems, with error rates that fall as fast as scores rise.

What all three agree on — which is what you should plan against
  • The models available today are already useful for a large set of real tasks, most of them involving reading and drafting.
  • Cost per unit of capability is falling fast (module 5.7), so build model-agnostically.
  • Data, evaluation, integration and governance are the bottleneck in almost every organisation, not model quality.
  • Reliability and verification are the hard engineering problems, and they are yours to solve rather than the vendor's.

Notice that every item on that list is something you can act on without resolving the disagreement — which is the practical point of presenting it.

The three questions that do most of the work

  1. Measured on what, and had the system seen it? (Module 7.4, 8.1.)
  2. Compared with what baseline? A person, or the simplest alternative — not with nothing. (Module 8.5.)
  3. Who reproduced it? A demo video is not a result; a published, repeatable evaluation on a defined set is.
And be fair in the other direction

Scepticism is not automatically rigour. “It is just autocomplete” is as unhelpful as “it is nearly human” — both are slogans standing in for a measurement. The same three questions apply to a dismissive claim: measured how, against what, reproduced by whom. The correct posture is neither belief nor dismissal but a small evaluation set of your own cases, which takes an afternoon and settles the argument for your specific task.

14.6 · Keeping up without drowning

What to follow

  • Primary sources — the model cards, system cards and technical reports from the labs. Dry, and the only place the caveats are.
  • One or two people who actually build things and publish evaluations, rather than commentary.
  • Your own evaluation set. When a new model appears, run it. Twenty minutes tells you more about its value to you than a month of reading.

What to ignore

  • Benchmark leaderboard movements of a few points.
  • Demo videos without a reproducible evaluation.
  • Anything predicting a specific capability at a specific date.
  • Both “this changes everything” and “this is a bubble”, in the same measure.

A sustainable cadence

  • Monthly: half an hour reading primary sources.
  • Quarterly: re-run your evaluation set against current models; re-check the cost figures in module 5, which move fast.
  • Annually: revisit architecture decisions, vendor terms and the governance list in module 10.

That is genuinely enough. The fundamentals in modules 2 to 4 have not changed since 2017.

The thing worth remembering from all fourteen modules

A neuron is a weighted sum and a threshold. A network is layers of them, trained by rolling downhill on an error surface. A language model is that machine, at enormous scale, predicting the next token. None of it understands your business, owns your risk, or knows what it does not know. It is a component — fast, broadly capable and unreliable in characterised ways — and you now know how to specify it, measure it, constrain it and budget for it. That is the whole job, and it is an engineering job.

  • Be genuinely evenhanded in 14.4. If the room can tell which position you hold, you have reduced a live technical question to an opinion, and you will be quoted on it.
  • The compounding chart in 14.2 is the single most useful slide for anyone being sold an autonomous agent. Draw it by hand if you have to.
  • Resist the urge to end on a prediction. Ending on “here is what to do regardless of who is right” is both more honest and more useful, and it sets up the capstone.
