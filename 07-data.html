Module 7 · Part II — Engineering it

Data

Five modules on models, and every one of them depended on something we kept deferring. This is the module that decides whether your project works — and it contains almost no mathematics, which is exactly why it gets skipped.

The sentence to take away

A model does not learn your intention. It learns your dataset — including every inconsistency, every shortcut, every accidental correlation and every disagreement between the people who labelled it. Everything in this module is a consequence of that one fact.

7.1 · Where the time actually goes

Ask a team that has shipped a machine-learning system how they spent the project, and the answer is consistently unglamorous. The modelling — the part that gets presented — is a minority of the effort.

A typical supervised vision project

Reference

Indicative rather than measured — ratios vary — but the ordering is remarkably stable across organisations. Note that the two largest bars are both data work, and neither requires a data scientist.

The good news

Most of this work does not need scarce specialists. It needs domain experts, a clear specification and project management — things you already have.

The bad news

It cannot be outsourced to a vendor, because the knowledge of what counts as a defect lives in your inspectors' heads. A supplier can build the model; only you can define the label.

The consequence

When someone proposes an AI project with a three-month timeline and no named data owner, the timeline is wrong. Ask who is going to label, and how many hours they have been allocated.

Why this matters more than the model

If you and inspector B disagree on 20% of parts, then no model trained on your combined labels can exceed about 80% agreement with either of you — not because the model is weak, but because the target is inconsistent. This is called the label noise ceiling, and it is the single most under-measured quantity in applied machine learning. Before funding a model, run exactly the exercise you just did with three of your own experts on 100 real parts. If they agree 95%, you have a viable project. If they agree 70%, you have a specification problem to fix first — and fixing it is cheaper and more valuable than any model.

  • Run this live with the audience voting by show of hands. The disagreement in the room is the demonstration, and it is far more persuasive than the statistic.
  • Cohen's kappa is worth explaining in one sentence: it is agreement corrected for the agreement you would get by chance. Two inspectors who both reject 90% of parts will agree 82% of the time by accident alone.
  • If somebody says “but the spec is objective” — point at the reveal table. The spec is objective; measuring a hairline crack by eye in four seconds is not.

The trap, stated plainly

At a 1% defect rate, a model that simply outputs “pass” for everything is 99% accurate. It would pass most reporting templates, and it detects nothing at all. If a supplier or an internal team quotes you an accuracy figure on an imbalanced problem without recall, they either do not understand the problem or they are hoping you do not.

What to ask for instead
  • Recall — what fraction of real defects did it catch?
  • Precision — of the parts it flagged, how many were really defective?
  • The confusion matrix, in absolute counts, on a realistic volume.
  • The operating point, and who chose it.

7.4 · Leakage: the 98% that means nothing

This is the most expensive mistake in this module, because it does not look like a mistake. The model reports excellent held-out accuracy, everyone signs off, and it fails in production.

Here is a dataset with a deliberate structure: 40 vehicles, 20 measurements each. The label depends only on which vehicle the measurement came from — there is no generalisable signal at all. A model that has seen other rows from the same vehicle can identify it and recite the answer. A model facing a genuinely new vehicle cannot do better than chance.

Where leakage actually comes from in real projects

Grouping leakage

Multiple photographs of the same part; several measurements from one engine; chunks of the same document; repeated visits by one customer. Split by the entity, never by the row.

Temporal leakage

Training on 2024 data and testing on 2023. In production you always predict forward, so evaluate forward: train on the past, test on the future, always.

Target leakage

A feature that is a consequence of the label rather than a cause. “Number of repair orders” predicts warranty failure superbly — and is only recorded after the failure. The classic killer: it survives every split strategy.

Preprocessing leakage

Scaling, imputing or selecting features using statistics computed over the whole dataset before splitting. Subtle, common, and it quietly inflates every result.

The two questions that catch nearly all of it

1. What is the unit that must not appear on both sides of the split? (A part, a vehicle, a customer, a document, a week.) 2. Would this feature have been available, with this value, at the moment the prediction is needed? If you make these two questions a standing item in every model review, you will catch most leakage before it reaches a steering committee.

7.5 · Drift: the model was right last year

A model is a photograph of a relationship at one moment. Suppliers change, tooling wears, sensors are recalibrated, a new product variant is introduced — and the incoming data stops resembling the training data. Accuracy decays silently, because nothing in the system knows the labels any more.

The population stability index is computed live over ten bins of the first feature. The conventional reading: below 0.1 no material change, 0.1–0.25 worth investigating, above 0.25 significant shift. It needs no labels, which is why it is the workhorse of production monitoring.

The operational point

You will usually not know accuracy has fallen, because labels arrive weeks late or never. So monitor the inputs, not just the outputs: distribution of every feature, rate of missing values, and the distribution of the model's own confidence scores. All three are free and all three move before the damage shows up.

Retraining is not a fix by itself

Retraining on recent data corrects gradual drift. It cannot correct a concept change — where the relationship itself is different, not just the inputs. And automatic retraining without evaluation is a way to deploy a worse model faster.

This applies to LLM systems too

Your prompts were tuned against one model version. The vendor updates it. Your documents are re-organised. Users start asking different questions. Same failure, different costume — which is why module 8 insists on a fixed evaluation set you re-run on every change.

Plan for at least one full re-labelling round. It is not a sign of failure — it is what happens when the first 500 labels reveal that the taxonomy was wrong, that “surface defect” needed to be four categories, or that two inspectors interpreted the guideline differently. Teams that budget for it ship; teams that do not quietly consume their model-development time doing it anyway.
