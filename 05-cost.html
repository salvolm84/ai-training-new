Module 5

The bill

Every idea in the previous four modules is arithmetic, and arithmetic costs electricity. This module puts numbers on it — training, inference, hardware, grid — and gives you a calculator you can point at your own use case.

Figures current as of September 2026 — verify before each delivery

How to read this module

Two things are true at the same time and both matter. Absolute energy use is large and growing fast. And the cost of a unit of useful work is collapsing — roughly tenfold per year for a fixed level of quality. Any presentation that gives you only one of those curves is selling you something. Sources are listed at the end; treat every number as order-of-magnitude, because the honest ones all carry wide error bars.

5.1 · The unit of work

One FLOP is one floating-point operation — a single multiply or add, exactly the kind you did by hand in module 2. It is the currency of this entire industry, and there is a remarkably simple rule connecting a model's size to the work of training it.

Training compute ≈ 6 × N × D N = number of parameters · D = number of training tokens

Where the 6 comes from: each parameter does roughly 2 operations in the forward pass (one multiply, one add) and roughly 4 in the backward pass, because backpropagation must compute gradients with respect to both the weights and the inputs. So every parameter costs about 6 operations for every token it sees. Inference is far cheaper: about 2 × N per token generated, since there is no backward pass.

Llama 3.1 405B

405 billion parameters × 15 trillion tokens × 6 ≈ 3.6×10²⁵ FLOP. Meta reported 30.8 million H100-hours; the rule of thumb reproduces that to within about 10%, which for a number this size is remarkable.

GPT-4 (2023)

Independently estimated at roughly 2×10²⁵ FLOP and about $80 million of compute. It was the first model publicly identified above 10²⁵; by mid-2025 more than thirty models had passed that mark.

Frontier runs, 2026

Reported estimates put current leading training runs between 10²⁶ and 10²⁷ FLOP, with compute costs in the hundreds of millions. Growth is now constrained by power and capital, not by chips.

  • Engineers like this section because it is the first time the whole field becomes dimensionally analysable. Lean into it: 6ND is the only equation they need to remember from the series.
  • Useful scale anchor: 10²⁵ FLOP is about the number of grains of sand on Earth, times ten million. Numbers that size only become tractable through the rest of this module's calculators.

The part nobody puts on the slide

The final run is a fraction of the true cost. Before it there are failed runs, ablations, hyperparameter searches and data pipeline work — commonly two to four times the headline number. Add research salaries and the compute is often no longer the largest line.

5.3 · Scaling laws: why models are the size they are

Given a fixed compute budget, you can spend it on a bigger model or on more data. For years the field over-spent on parameters. In 2022 the “Chinchilla” result showed the compute-optimal ratio is roughly 20 training tokens per parameter — and that many famous large models had been significantly under-trained for their size.

Why this matters commercially, not just academically

Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal. If a model will serve a billion requests, it is worth deliberately over-training a smaller model far past the Chinchilla point: you pay more once, then save on every inference forever. That is why the useful models of the last two years have been getting smaller and better rather than simply bigger — and why a 2026 8-billion-parameter model outperforms a 2020 175-billion-parameter one.

And the wall nobody has solved

The rule needs 20 tokens per parameter. A 10-trillion-parameter model would need 200 trillion tokens of quality text, which is more high-quality human text than exists in accessible form. That data ceiling — not chip supply — is one of the main reasons the field has pivoted towards spending compute at answer time (reasoning models) instead of at training time.

5.4 · Inference is the real bill

Training is a single dramatic expense. Inference is a small expense repeated continuously, and at corporate scale it wins. The industry consensus is that inference now accounts for the clear majority of AI compute.

Per-request comparison

Reference points: Google published a median Gemini text prompt at 0.24 Wh of energy, 0.03 g CO₂e and 0.26 mL of water (measured May 2025, published August 2025). Independent estimates for a typical ChatGPT query cluster around 0.3 Wh. Reasoning models and image generation are one to two orders of magnitude higher, because they generate far more tokens per answer.

The efficiency curve is steep

Google reported that the median Gemini prompt used 33 times less energy in May 2025 than a year earlier, for a better answer. Quantisation, distillation, mixture-of-experts routing, better batching and custom silicon compound quickly. Any per-query figure older than about a year is wrong by an order of magnitude.

And total demand still rises

Efficiency per request fell 33× while total AI electricity use grew sharply, because volume grew faster. This is Jevons' paradox: making something cheaper increases total consumption. Both statements are true and neither cancels the other.

The honest comparison

At 0.3 Wh, one request is roughly ten seconds of a desktop PC, or a fifth of the energy in one minute of video streaming. The unsettling number is never the single request — it is the aggregate, and the datacenters being built for it.

Why liquid cooling stopped being optional

A conventional enterprise rack draws 5–15 kW and is air-cooled. An air-cooled H100 rack is around 40 kW. A current-generation GB200 NVL72 rack draws roughly 120 kW in the footprint of a wardrobe. Above about 30 kW per rack, moving that heat with air stops being physically sensible, so direct liquid cooling becomes the design baseline. This is a plumbing and civil-engineering change, not an IT upgrade — which is why AI capacity is constrained by construction and grid connections rather than by chip availability.

5.6 · The macro picture

Data centres — all of them, not only AI — consumed around 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5% of global electricity. The International Energy Agency's base case has that roughly doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030, around 3% of world demand, with AI-specific consumption tripling over the period.

IEA base case

IEA, Energy and AI (2025). Grey bars are reference points for scale, not forecasts. Note the honest caveat the IEA itself makes: projections this steep depend on assumptions about efficiency, chip supply and buildout that could plausibly move the 2030 figure by ±40%.

In perspective

945 TWh is roughly three times Italy's entire annual electricity consumption. It is also only about 3% of global demand — smaller than industrial motors, cement or air conditioning. Both framings are used in public debate, usually without the other.

The local problem

The difficulty is not the global percentage, it is the concentration. A single campus can request 300–1,000 MW at one grid node — comparable to a small city — and grid connection queues, not construction, are now the binding constraint in most markets.

Water

Evaporative cooling consumes water. Google reported 0.26 mL per median text prompt, which is trivial individually and material at campus scale in a water-stressed region. Closed-loop liquid cooling largely removes the consumption, at higher capital cost and slightly worse energy efficiency.

5.7 · The other curve

If you present only the energy chart, you will mislead the room. The price of a fixed level of capability has been falling at roughly an order of magnitude per year — one of the fastest cost declines recorded for any technology.

Price for a fixed quality bar

Log scale

Reported API list prices for the cheapest model matching “GPT-4-class” quality at each date; order-of-magnitude, and the definition of the quality bar is necessarily loose. Compiled from public pricing and industry analyses (a16z's “LLMflation” analysis puts the rate at approximately 10× per year for a constant capability level).

Distillation

Train a small model to imitate a large one. Much of the quality at a fraction of the size — the main driver of cheap capable models.

Quantisation

Store weights at 8 or 4 bits instead of 16. Two to four times less memory and faster arithmetic, for a small accuracy loss.

Mixture of experts

Activate only a small fraction of a huge model per token. Capacity of a large model at the running cost of a small one.

Better silicon

Each GPU generation brings roughly 2–3× more useful throughput per watt for this specific workload, plus purpose-built inference chips.

What this means for planning

Do not architect around today's price. A use case that is marginal at current cost will likely be comfortable within 18 months, and a system built to be model-agnostic — swap the model, keep the retrieval, the evaluation set and the guard-rails — captures that decline for free. Conversely, do not build a business case that depends on the decline continuing: the constraint is shifting from silicon to electricity, and electricity prices do not fall 10× per year.

5.8 · Buy or build: the decision you will actually face

Pay per token through an API, or run open-weight models on your own hardware. The crossover is a volume question, and the answer is usually less obvious than either vendor claims.

Self-hosted cost = capital amortised over 36 months + electricity at €120/MWh with PUE 1.2 + 35% for hosting, networking and operations. It excludes the engineering team, which is usually the real cost and is why small deployments almost never win on price.

Reasons to self-host that are not about price

  • Data residency and confidentiality — the decisive argument in most regulated or IP-sensitive contexts, and it does not need a cost justification.
  • Version stability. A hosted model can change under you; a local one cannot. This matters enormously for anything validated.
  • Latency for real-time or on-premise industrial use.
  • Availability independent of a vendor's capacity, rate limits or terms.

Reasons the API usually wins anyway

  • Utilisation. Your own GPUs idle overnight and at weekends; you paid for them anyway. At 25% utilisation you are paying four times the theoretical unit cost.
  • The frontier moves. Hardware bought today serves a model that will be two generations old in eighteen months.
  • Quality gap. The best open-weight models trail the best hosted ones — the gap has narrowed a lot, but it is not zero.
  • Staffing. Serving infrastructure at reliable production quality is a specialist team, permanently.
The pattern that actually gets deployed

Hybrid, and it is not a compromise. Route the high-volume, well-defined, sensitive tasks — classification, extraction, redaction, retrieval embeddings — to a small self-hosted model where volume and confidentiality both favour it. Route the low-volume, hard, open-ended work to the best frontier API available. Keep retrieval, evaluation and guard-rails outside the model entirely, so that swapping either side is a configuration change rather than a project.

5.9 · What to take back to your desk

Five questions worth asking about any AI proposal

  1. What is the token volume? Everything — cost, latency, feasibility — follows from it, and most proposals have never estimated it.
  2. Is this inference or training? If someone proposes training a model, ask what retrieval and prompting achieved first.
  3. What is the measured baseline? Accuracy on which held-out data, against what a person currently achieves.
  4. What happens when it is wrong? If the answer involves safety, certification or money leaving the company, the design needs a human in the path.
  5. Is it model-agnostic? If swapping the model is a rewrite, you have locked yourself out of a tenfold annual cost decline.

What Part I was about

A neuron is a weighted sum and a threshold. A network is layers of them, trained by rolling downhill on an error surface. A language model is that machine, at enormous scale, doing one thing: predicting the next token. And the reason it works now rather than in 1969 is that the arithmetic became affordable.

Which means the useful questions in your job are rarely about the mathematics. They are about data you own, errors you can tolerate, decisions that need a human, and the size of the bill. Those are engineering and management questions — and they are what Part II is about.
