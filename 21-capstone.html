Module 21 · Part IV — Shipping it

Capstone

Fourteen modules of general material. This one produces something: a specific use case from your own work, run through everything the course covered, with an honest scorecard and three concrete next steps.

How to run this session

Teams of three to four, each bringing one real candidate use case. Forty minutes filling the canvas, twenty minutes presenting to the room, fifteen minutes plotting everything on the portfolio grid. The deliverable is not a decision — it is a short list of pre-qualified ideas with their gaps named, which is what the person sponsoring this series actually wanted.

21.3 · The portfolio

Every use case you have saved, plotted. Bubble size is risk. This is the picture to take to the sponsor — not the individual canvases.

Value against feasibility

Save several, then look at the shape

Top right — do these

High value, feasible now. There are usually fewer than people expect, and they are usually less exciting than the ones in the top left.

Top left — fix the gap

Worth doing and not yet possible. The blocker is almost always data or a written target definition. That is the work to fund, and it is cheaper than a model.

Bottom right — quick wins

Easy and modest. Take one or two to build capability and credibility, then stop. A portfolio of only these is a hobby.

Bottom left — say no

Low value, not feasible. Saying so explicitly, with the canvas as evidence, is one of the more valuable things this course can do for your organisation.

21.4 · Workshop agenda

75 minutes

For the facilitator
Two facilitation warnings

First, the room will want to discuss solutions and you must keep them on the problem until the data section is filled in — otherwise every canvas becomes “we should use an LLM”. Second, protect the honesty of the data and risk answers. The scorecard is only useful if people tick “no written definition” when that is true, and there is social pressure not to. Say explicitly that a low readiness score is a useful finding, not a failed canvas.

21.5 · Closing the series

What we covered

  • Part I — what the machine is: a weighted sum, a training loop, a next-token predictor, and its electricity bill.
  • Part II — how to engineer with it: the right algorithm for the data, the data itself, honest evaluation, agents and their permissions, and the law.
  • Part III — how it lands: engineering applications, prompting as a skill, the human factors that decide adoption, and the limits.
  • Part IV — how it ships: telemetry and time series, honest uncertainty, explanations, adapting models, running them in production, the safety case on the vehicle, and your own use case.

The four sentences worth remembering

  1. It is all weighted sums and one loop. Nothing in any of this is magic, and knowing that is what lets you specify it.
  2. The model learns your dataset, not your intention. Most failures trace back to this.
  3. A system without an evaluation set is an anecdote. Build the test before the thing.
  4. The bottleneck is never the model. It is data, integration, review capacity and who is accountable — all of which are yours.
And the one instruction

Pick the use case in the top-right of your portfolio grid. Write its evaluation set first — thirty real cases with expected answers, which takes an afternoon. Then build the smallest thing that could pass it. You will learn more from that afternoon than from any further reading, and you will have done the single step that most AI projects skip.

  • Collect the canvases. Even half-completed, they are the most accurate picture of AI readiness in the organisation that anyone will produce this year, and they are evidence for the AI inventory in module 10.9.
  • Expect two or three genuinely good ideas from a room of twenty, and expect at least one to be something nobody in the AI programme had thought of, because the people with the problems are in the room and the people with the technology were guessing.
  • End by telling people what happens next and who decides. A capstone that produces a list nobody actions is worse than no capstone, because it teaches the room that this was theatre.
  • Finally: record attendance across the series. It is your AI-literacy evidence under module 10.9, and it is the easiest compliance box any of us will ever tick.
