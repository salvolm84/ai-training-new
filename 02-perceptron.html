Module 2

The perceptron

One artificial neuron, with the lid off. Everything in this module is two multiplications, one addition and a threshold — and by the end of it you will have trained one by hand, and then watched it train itself.

2.1 · What is inside a neuron

A neuron takes some numbers in, multiplies each one by its own weight, adds them up, adds a constant called the bias, and pushes the result through a small non-linear function. That is the complete specification. A model with 400 billion parameters is this, 400 billion numbers' worth.

z = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b    →    output = f(z)

The two inputs x₁, x₂ come from the outside world — a sensor reading, a pixel, the output of another neuron. The weights and the bias are the parameters: the only things training is allowed to change. Note what the weights mean physically: a large positive weight says “this input matters and pushes towards yes”; a negative weight says “this input argues against”; a weight near zero says “ignore this input”. The bias is how easily the neuron fires at all, independent of any input.

The geometric picture — this is the whole module

With two inputs, w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0 is the equation of a straight line. The neuron says “yes” on one side of that line and “no” on the other. The weights rotate the line; the bias slides it. Training a neuron means positioning a line. That is all it can ever do — and the whole of module 3 exists because of that limitation.

2.3 · The wall

You cannot solve XOR. Not because you are not trying hard enough — it is provably impossible, and the proof is one sentence: the two “yes” cases sit on opposite corners of the square, so no single straight line can separate them from the two “no” cases. Go back and look at the plane with XOR selected. There is no line.

Why this mattered so much

In 1969 Minsky and Papert published exactly this result. Their conclusion was narrow and correct: a single-layer perceptron is limited to linearly separable problems. But XOR is trivial, and if the celebrated learning machine could not do trivial, the case for funding collapsed.

Neural network research went quiet for most of the following two decades. The solution — add a layer of neurons in the middle — was already understood. What nobody had was a practical way to train the middle layer. That arrived in 1986, and it is called backpropagation.

The fix, in one picture

XOR is separable if you are allowed two lines instead of one: one line saying “at least one input is on”, another saying “not both are on”, and a third neuron combining them. Three neurons in two layers solve XOR exactly.

Each added layer lets the network fold the space again. Module 3 makes this concrete — and you will watch XOR get solved.

Key takeaway

A single neuron draws one straight boundary. Depth is what buys curvature. Every time you hear “deep” learning, the thing depth is buying is the ability to carve a complicated shape out of the input space instead of slicing it once.

  • Let them fail at XOR for a full minute before you explain. The frustration is the lesson, and the room will remember it for the rest of the series.
  • Good analogy: one neuron is a single guillotine cut through the data. If the thing you want is two diagonal corners, no single cut gets it.
  • Honest footnote: Minsky and Papert were right about the maths and wrong about the implication. Worth saying — it is a good example of how a correct result can stall a field for a generation.

2.4 · Now let it learn

Rosenblatt's 1958 contribution was not the neuron — that was 1943. It was the learning rule: a procedure by which the machine corrects its own weights from its own mistakes. It is three lines long.

for each example (x, target):
  prediction = f(w·x + b)
  error = target − prediction
  w ← w + η · error · x    (η = learning rate)
  b ← b + η · error

Read the update rule in words: if the answer was too low, push the weights in the direction of the input; if it was too high, pull them away. Inputs that were large get a large correction; inputs that were zero get none, because they cannot have contributed. Nothing more than that — and it is guaranteed to find a solution in finite time whenever one exists.

Try the learning rate

Set η to 0.01 and run: correct, but slow. Set it to 1.0: it lurches, overshoots and sometimes takes longer than the small value. This trade-off never goes away — the learning rate is still the single most important knob when training a model with billions of parameters.

2.5 · The activation function

The threshold at the end of the neuron seems like a detail. It is load-bearing: without a non-linear function there, a stack of layers collapses into a single layer, because a linear function of a linear function is still linear. Depth would buy nothing at all.

Solid: the function. Dashed: its slope (derivative) — which is what training needs, and where things go wrong.

Why ReLU took over

Look at the dashed line for the sigmoid: away from zero it is almost flat. A flat slope means “this weight barely affects the error”, so training grinds to a halt — the vanishing gradient problem that made deep networks untrainable for years. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for all positive inputs, so the signal passes through unattenuated, and it costs one comparison to compute. A change this crude and this cheap is a large part of why deep learning started working.

2.6 · How real training works: rolling downhill

The perceptron rule only works for a neuron with a hard threshold and separable data. Everything modern uses the same idea in a smoother form. Define a loss — one number measuring total wrongness — then repeatedly compute which direction reduces it, and step that way.

w ← w − η · ∂Loss/∂w     “move each weight against the slope of the error”

Go deeper · what changes at scale

Everything above is one weight on one loss curve. A real model has billions of weights, so the loss surface has billions of dimensions and you cannot draw it. Three practical differences:

  • Stochastic gradient descent. Computing the slope over the entire dataset is far too expensive, so it is estimated from a small random batch. The estimate is noisy — and the noise turns out to help, by shaking the model out of bad valleys.
  • Momentum and adaptive rates. Optimisers like Adam keep a running average of recent gradients and give each weight its own effective step size. This is why you rarely tune a single learning rate by hand any more; you tune a schedule.
  • High dimensions are kinder than they look. In billions of dimensions, a point where every direction goes uphill is vanishingly rare. Most flat spots are saddle points with an escape route, which is why training enormous models works far better than the two-valley picture above would suggest.

What does not change: the loop. Forward pass, measure the error, compute the slope, step. A €200 million training run is that loop, a very large number of times.

  • The blindfolded-hiker analogy works well: you cannot see the valley, you can only feel the slope under your boots, and you take a step. The learning rate is your stride length.
  • Connect back to 2.4: the perceptron rule is gradient descent, on a loss that happens to be non-smooth. Same loop, cruder measurement.
  • Land the transition to module 3 here: “we now have a neuron and a way to train it. The only thing between us and a modern model is stacking them — and one more idea for sharing the blame.”
