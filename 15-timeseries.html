Module 15 · Part IV — Shipping it

Time series and telemetry

Most of the data a car produces is not a table of independent rows. It is a stream: brake temperatures, cell voltages, wheel speeds, vibration, sampled hundreds or thousands of times a second. Almost every technique from Parts I–III still applies — once you have respected the one thing that makes streams different. Ignore it and your validation scores will lie to you.

The sentence to take away

In a time series, neighbouring samples are not independent evidence — they are mostly the same evidence measured twice. Every method in this module, from resampling to validation to anomaly detection, is a way of respecting that fact instead of being fooled by it.

15.1 · Why order changes everything

Nearly all of the machine learning in modules 2–8 quietly assumes that rows are independent and identically distributed — i.i.d. Shuffle the rows and nothing is lost; split them at random and the test set is a fair sample of the future. Telemetry breaks both halves of that assumption. Independent fails because a brake disc at 612 °C now will be at roughly 612 °C a tenth of a second from now. Identically distributed fails because the car on lap 18 — lighter on fuel, older tyres, hotter cooling system — is not the car on lap 2.

The practical consequence is that the number of rows in your logger file says very little about how much information you have. The lab makes that concrete with a simulated front-left brake disc over eight laps.

Effective sample size is estimated as n / (1 + 2·Σρk), summing the autocorrelation until it first drops below 0.05. It is an estimate, not a law — but it is the right order of magnitude, and the order of magnitude is the point.

Logging faster does not buy information

Going from 1 Hz to 10 Hz multiplies the rows by ten and the effective sample size by roughly nothing. The extra samples are needed to see fast events — a lock-up, a spike — but they do not make a statistical result ten times more certain. Error bars computed as if the rows were independent will be far too narrow.

Shuffling destroys the thing you want

After shuffling, the autocorrelation collapses and every row looks independent. That is exactly what a random train/test split does behind your back — it treats yesterday's samples and tomorrow's as interchangeable. Section 15.5 shows what that does to a validation score.

  • About 5 minutes. Start at 1 Hz, read out the effective sample size, then slide to 10 Hz and ask the room to guess the new number before you read it. Most people guess ten times larger.
  • Point at the bump in the autocorrelation at one lap: the disc at turn 1 this lap looks like the disc at turn 1 last lap. That periodicity is what section 15.4 exploits for forecasting.
  • Expected question: "so is high-rate logging pointless?" No — you need the rate to resolve fast events and to avoid aliasing (next section). You just cannot count the rows as evidence.

15.2 · Different clocks: resampling, alignment and fusion

A test car does not have one sample rate. It has dozens, each chosen by a different supplier for a different reason, and none of them share a clock by default.

1 kHz +

Accelerometers, vibration

Fast enough to resolve driveline orders and bearing tones. Timestamped by the logger, usually well. Noisy, and integrating it to get speed or position drifts within seconds.

10–100 Hz

CAN signals

Wheel speeds, torque requests, pedal positions. Each message has its own cycle time and jitter, and its timestamp is when the logger received it — not when the ECU measured it.

10 Hz

GNSS / GPS

Absolute position and speed that do not drift — but coarse, noisy, delayed by the receiver's own filtering, and absent under a bridge or in a pit garage.

Before any model sees this data, it has to land on a common time grid. There are two ways to get that badly wrong: resample carelessly and invent frequencies that were never there, or trust the timestamps and combine signals that are offset by a few hundred milliseconds — at 70 m/s, a 200 ms offset is fourteen metres of track.

Aliasing is not a cosmetic problem. A model trained on the 50 Hz “every Nth sample” version will learn a 13 Hz oscillation that does not exist, and a frequency feature built on it (section 15.3) will report energy in exactly the wrong place. Most logging tools and dataframe libraries resample by picking or by interpolation, neither of which filters. Check which one yours does.

Alignment and fusion

Once the signals share a grid, the next question is whether they share a clock. The usual answer is “nearly”, and the remedy is to estimate the offset from the data — slide one signal against another until they agree best — rather than trust the timestamps. Then you can do something genuinely useful: fuse them. The accelerometer is fast and smooth but drifts; GPS does not drift but is slow, noisy and late. A Kalman filter uses each to correct the other's weakness.

  • Expected question: "is a Kalman filter machine learning?" Not in the usual sense — it is a model-based estimator from the 1960s. But it is the right tool here, and feeding its output to an ML model is far better than feeding raw, misaligned channels.

15.3 · Windows and features

Classical models do not take a stream; they take rows. The standard bridge is a sliding window: cut the signal into overlapping chunks, compute a handful of numbers from each — mean, standard deviation, RMS, peak, slope, and energy in particular frequency bands — and each window becomes one row. A gradient-boosted model on good window features is the baseline every sequence model in section 15.7 has to beat, and it is a hard baseline.

The choice that matters most is the window length, because it trades time resolution against frequency resolution. A window of N samples at rate fs resolves frequencies fs/N apart, and blurs anything that happens within N/fs seconds. You cannot have both. The lab is a wheel-bearing defect developing on a hub accelerometer, hidden under broadband road noise.

Simulated at 2,048 Hz for 20 s: shaft order at 24 Hz and its harmonic, gear mesh at 552 Hz, broadband noise, and two patches of rough road (shaded) that raise the noise 2.6-fold. From 8 s a defect tone at 98.4 Hz (4.1 × shaft speed) and its harmonic grow slowly. Each window is Hann-weighted and transformed with an FFT computed in your browser; windows overlap by half. “Flags fault at” is the first time a feature exceeds its highest value during the healthy 0–8 s — the earliest detection possible with zero false alarms on this run.

Why RMS fails and band energy works

The defect adds a few percent to total vibration energy, while rough road adds several hundred percent. Any feature that sums over all frequencies is dominated by the road. A feature that looks only where the physics says the defect will be — a band around the bearing's defect frequency, tracking shaft speed — ignores the road almost entirely. This is domain knowledge doing the work that people expect the model to do.

Features also leak

A centred rolling mean uses samples from the future. A feature normalised by the whole-session mean uses the whole session. Both are fine for offline analysis and both are leakage if the model will run live. Every window feature should be computable from data available at the moment the prediction is needed — the second question in module 7.4.

  • About 7 minutes. Start at 64 samples: lines 32 Hz apart, the defect is smeared across a band nearly 100 Hz wide, and the band feature only flags it late. Go to 1024 and the peak stands out within a couple of seconds of onset — then point out the cost: each window is half a second, so a fast event would be blurred. RMS never flags it at any window length.
  • Move the inspect slider into the shaded rough-road patch. The whole spectrum rises; the defect band barely changes in relative terms. That is the core of the argument for physics-guided features.
  • Expected question: "doesn't a neural network learn these features itself?" Given enough labelled examples, yes — see 15.7. With a few dozen failed bearings, it learns the road surface instead.

15.4 · Forecasting: beat the naive baseline first

A forecast is only worth something relative to what you could have predicted for free. Time series have two free predictors that are embarrassingly hard to beat, and no forecasting result should be presented without both:

  • Persistence — the next value will be the same as the last one. Optimal for a random walk, and surprisingly good for anything slow.
  • Seasonal naive — the value will be the same as one cycle ago: this point on the previous lap, this hour last week.

What to ask for in every forecasting review

Skill relative to the naive baselines, at each horizon you care about, on a period after the training data. A model reporting 4 °C mean error sounds good until you learn that “same as last lap” gets 4.3 °C. The standard scaled metric (MASE) divides the model's error by the naive error for exactly this reason: below 1 you beat it, above 1 you should have shipped the baseline.

  • About 7 minutes. Start on the brake series: “same point last lap” is very strong, and the fitted model barely matches it. That is the result, and it is a perfectly good one — it tells you not to build the model.
  • Switch to depot charging: daily and weekly cycles plus a trend, and here the model does earn a clear margin by combining last week, yesterday and the last few hours.
  • Then the drift series: there is no season, the best forecast is essentially the last value, and the model cannot do better because there is nothing to learn. Saying that out loud is honest work.
  • Expected question: "what about deep forecasting models?" Large forecasting competitions (the M4 and M5 series) found that simple statistical methods and well-tuned gradient boosting are very hard to beat on ordinary business series. Section 15.7 covers when deep models do pay.

15.5 · The time-leakage trap

Module 7.4 showed grouping leakage: rows from the same vehicle on both sides of a split. Time series have the same disease in a form that is harder to see, because there are no obvious groups — every sample is simply very similar to its neighbours. A random k-fold split puts sample t in test and samples t−1 and t+1 in training, and a flexible model learns to answer by looking next door.

The dataset: one row per fast-charge event on a development battery pack, predicting the cell temperature rise. Requested charge current genuinely drives it. A hidden, unmeasured factor — coolant fouling and cell ageing — also drives it, wanders slowly and trends upward. Two other sensors, ambient temperature and pump duty, drift slowly but are unrelated to the target. They become the model's clock.

1,000 charge events. The first 750 are the development set, where all four validation schemes run; the last 250 are “production”, scored once with a model trained on all 750. Random k-fold

Flattering whenever samples are autocorrelated. On telemetry, assume it is wrong unless someone has shown otherwise. It is still the default in most libraries.

Blocked, with a gap

Contiguous folds remove most of the neighbour effect; an embargo gap either side of each test block removes the rest. Still trains on the future to test on the past — acceptable for some questions, not for forecasting.

Walk-forward

Train on the past, test on the next block, roll forward. It mirrors how the model will actually be used. Make the test blocks as long as the period you will run between retrains.

  • About 8 minutes. This is the section to be firm about. Read the random k-fold number, then the production number, and let the gap sit for a moment.
  • Move k from 1 to 25: the gap between random k-fold and production persists whatever the model's flexibility. This is a property of the split, not of the model.
  • Shorten the half-life to the minimum: neighbours stop sharing the hidden factor and the four schemes move much closer to each other. Production stays worse, because the upward trend carries it outside anything seen in training — that is the envelope problem of module 11.5, a separate issue.
  • Walk-forward is closer to production but often still optimistic, because production runs further past the training data than one test block does. That is honest, and worth saying.
  • Expected question: "we use cross-validation from the library — is that random?" Almost certainly, unless someone chose a time-series splitter on purpose. Ask to see the line of code.

15.6 · Sliding-window anomaly detection

Module 6.6 introduced anomaly detection: learn what normal looks like, flag what is not. On a stream the simplest serious version is a rolling z-score — compare each sample with the mean and spread of a window of recent history, and alarm when it sits too many standard deviations away for long enough. Three knobs, and every one of them is a trade-off between false alarms and missed faults.

The data: one cell's voltage minus the pack median, over a 24-lap battery endurance session. Three real faults are injected, of three different shapes. Two benign events are included too, because real sessions contain them: a qualifying push where cells heat unevenly, and a pit stop where the load disappears.

Faults: an intermittent connector (six dips of 2–4 s), a balancing circuit stuck on (a 5 mV step for 150 s), and an internal micro-short (a slow 0.008 mV/s decline from 1,750 s). A fault counts as caught only if an alarm overlaps it and the same detector, with its threshold half a σ looser, stays silent there when run on the identical session with the faults removed — so a noise alarm that happens to land inside a fault is scored as a false alarm, not a catch.

The window decides which faults exist

A short trailing window sees spikes and adapts to everything slow, so a slow drift is invisible to it by construction — the baseline drifts with the fault. A long or frozen baseline sees the drift, but it also sees every benign change of operating condition. Real monitoring systems run several detectors at different time scales, and compare each cell with its siblings rather than with its own past.

Tuned on one session is tuned to one session

Run the brute-force search, apply the best zero-false-alarm row, then press New session. Same faults, same car, new noise — and the false-alarm count usually moves. Thresholds chosen on the same data you report them on are the anomaly-detection version of leakage: tune on some sessions, report on others.

  • About 7 minutes. Hand the sliders to the room: “catch all three, zero false alarms”. Someone will get two. Then run the search and show what is actually achievable — and at what cost.
  • The pit stop is the instructive false alarm: with a short window, the noise collapses at zero load, the estimated spread shrinks, and the cold cell's ordinary sag as the load returns looks like a fault.
  • Set the frozen baseline to show the opposite failure: the micro-short is caught, and so is the qualifying push, which is perfectly healthy.
  • Expected question: "would ML fix this?" Partly: a model of normal behaviour given load and temperature removes the benign events from the residual. It is still a threshold on a residual, and every trade-off on this page still applies to it.

15.7 · When deep sequence models earn their keep

Recurrent networks (LSTM, GRU), one-dimensional convolutional networks and transformers all take a sequence directly and learn their own features. They are sometimes the right answer. They are not the default answer, and on most engineering telemetry problems a gradient-boosted model on well-chosen window features is faster to build, easier to validate, cheaper to run and at least as accurate.

In a product, that is not good enough: a range-to-empty estimate of “42 km” means something very different at ±2 km than at ±20.
