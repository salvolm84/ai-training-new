1.1 · Eighty years in one line

Key takeaway

The neuron model is from 1943. Backpropagation, the training algorithm behind every modern network, was popularised in 1986. The transformer, which underpins every large language model, is from 2017. The field advances in long plateaus punctuated by short jumps — and the jumps have nearly always come from a new kind of hardware or a new pile of data, not from a new theory.

  • Good opening line: “everything I am going to show you today was invented before most of this room finished school. The reason it works now is that we can afford it.”
  • Linger on 1969 (Minsky & Papert) — it sets up module 2's XOR wall, which you will hit deliberately.
  • Linger on 2012 (AlexNet): GPUs were designed to draw triangles for video games. That accident of hardware economics is why deep learning happened when it did.

1.2 · AI, ML, deep learning, GenAI — what contains what

These five words are used interchangeably in most meetings and they are not interchangeable. They nest: each one is a subset of the one outside it.

Where the confusion costs money

“We need AI for this” is not a requirement. A thermostat with a lookup table is AI by the 1956 definition and needs no data at all; a large language model is AI too and needs a datacenter. The useful question is never “should we use AI” but “which of these five things, and what does it need from us?”

1.3 · Three ways to solve the same problem

Take one concrete task: decide whether a weld in an inspection photograph is defective. Here is the same job done three ways. The differences are not about sophistication — they are about where the knowledge lives and who is accountable for it.

Rule-based

A human writes the logic

if crack_length > 2.0 mm and contrast > 0.4: reject()

Knowledge lives in: the head of the engineer who wrote the rule, then in the source code.

Needs: a specification. No data.

Strength: fully auditable. You can explain any decision line by line and certify it.

Weakness: every new case is a new rule. Systems with 4,000 rules become impossible to change safely.

Classical machine learning

The machine infers the logic from examples

model.fit( X = 50,000 labelled photos, y = pass / reject )

Knowledge lives in: the numbers (weights) learned from the data.

Needs: a large set of examples, each already labelled by a human expert.

Strength: catches patterns nobody could write down, including ones the expert cannot articulate.

Weakness: it is only as good, and as biased, as the labels. It will not tell you why.

Generative AI

A general model is instructed in words

“Here is an inspection photo and our acceptance standard. Assess the weld and draft the NCR report.”

Knowledge lives in: a model someone else pre-trained on an enormous general corpus, plus your instructions and documents at the moment of asking.

Needs: almost no training data — but good prompts, good context, and review.

Strength: handles the unstructured part (language, reports, edge cases) and needs no project to get started.

Weakness: confident when wrong, non-deterministic, and its reasoning is not a certificate.

In practice a real system is usually all three at once: a learned model scores the image, hard rules act as guard-rails on the output (“never auto-accept a safety-critical weld”), and a language model writes the report and answers the inspector's questions.

  • Ask the room: which of the three would you be willing to put in front of a regulator? That question usually does more for understanding than any definition.
  • Useful framing: rules are deductive (general → specific), ML is inductive (specific → general). Engineers get this instantly.
